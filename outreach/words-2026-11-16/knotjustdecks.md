### Composite or wood

Composite boards are made from recycled materials and durable polymers. They won't splinter or rot, they don't need yearly staining, and they come in many colors, textures and finishes. We build with Trex, whose boards are made in the USA and carry a 25-year limited fade and stain warranty. "Limited" means it has conditions, so read the terms.

Wood is the classic choice: pressure-treated lumber, which is wood treated to resist rot and insects, plus cedar and specialty woods. Wood often costs less to start and asks more of you afterward, usually regular cleaning and a fresh coat of stain or sealer every few years. Composite usually costs more up front and asks less. Many homeowners pick wood for its natural warmth and look, and we walk you through the pros and cons before you decide.

### Railings, lighting and stairs

Railing style changes the look of a deck more than most people expect, and railings and stairs are also safety parts. Many building codes ask for a railing once a deck is about 30 inches or more above the ground, and stairs have rules for step height and handrails, so these choices usually involve your permit office as well as your taste. Lighting along the stairs and the edges makes evening use safer and lets you use the deck after dark.

### Footings built for Fort Wayne winters

Footings are the concrete piers that carry a deck's weight into the ground. When they aren't deep enough, they can shift as the ground freezes and thaws, and that can twist the whole structure. Footings usually need to reach below the frost line, the depth where the ground freezes in winter, which is commonly about three feet in northern Indiana. Your local permit office sets the exact number. Footing depth is one of the common deck mistakes, and one of the costly ones.

### Drainage and board spacing

Water that pools under a deck can rot the framing, wash out soil and cause trouble near your foundation. Boards set too tight or too far apart can warp, buckle and trap moisture that speeds decay. Both wood and composite expand and contract with temperature and moisture, so installers leave small gaps between boards, and the right gap depends on the material and the manufacturer's directions. We build with manufacturer-approved methods and plan ventilation and drainage into every deck.

### Replacing an old deck

Boards are the cheap part of an old deck. Rotted posts, rotted beams or a weak connection to the house are not, and when the framing has failed, rebuilding is usually safer than patching it. A rebuild often needs the same footings and permits as a new deck, so it helps to treat it as a new project.

### Your roof, checked before you decide

Every roofing project begins with a free inspection, so you understand the condition of your roof before you make any decisions. An inspection usually looks at the shingles for cracks, curling and missing pieces, at the granules (the gritty coating that protects shingles) and at the flashing, the metal strips that seal joints around chimneys, vents and walls. It also looks at the vents, the gutters and, when it can, the attic for signs of moisture. A leak can show up inside a long way from where the water gets in, so a good inspection follows the water.

### Repair or replace

A repair usually makes sense when the damage is limited to one area and the rest of the roof has years left. Replacement usually makes more sense when the roof is near the end of its life, when leaks keep returning in different places, or when damage covers a large part of a slope. The written proposal lays out our recommendation, the materials, the price and the scope of the work.

### After a storm

Northeast Indiana gets heavy snow, high winds, hail and driving rain, and storms can damage a roof in ways that aren't always visible from the ground. Hail can bruise shingles and knock off their protective granules without leaving a visible hole. Wind can crease, lift or tear off shingles, and fallen branches can break them. Any of these can let water in later, which is why a roof can look fine from the street and still need work. We inspect your roof and help work out whether repairs or a full replacement are needed.

### Insurance claims

We assist homeowners with insurance claims by providing thorough documentation and working with you through the process. A claims adjuster, the person who inspects the damage and estimates the repair, usually visits, and photos with the date of the storm plus a written description tend to matter. Your deductible, the part of the cost you pay yourself, is set by your policy.

### Emergency tarping

When a storm leaves your roof open, we offer emergency tarping. A tarp is a heavy waterproof sheet fastened over the damaged area to keep water out until a permanent repair can be made. Photograph the damage before it goes on and keep your receipts, because many policies reimburse reasonable temporary repairs. A repair in deep winter can also be temporary, because the sealing strips that hold shingles down bond best in mild weather. A roof that is leaking now shouldn't wait, though, and a tarp can hold things until the permanent work is done.

### Shingles and metal roofing

We install CertainTeed architectural asphalt shingles, which are thicker and layered, so a roof has more depth than flat shingles give. We also install metal roofing, and we use U.S.-made products whenever possible. Both are durable options that suit different budgets and long-term goals, and the written proposal names the one we recommend for your house.

### How a roofing job runs

1. **Free roof inspection.** We inspect your roof to judge its condition, find damage and decide the best solution.
2. **Written proposal.** You get a detailed proposal with our recommendations, the materials, the pricing and the project scope.
3. **Planning and scheduling.** Once you approve, we coordinate the materials, permits when they are required and the schedule, and we answer your questions before installation starts.
4. **Installation.** Our crew removes the damaged roofing as needed and installs your new roofing system.
5. **Final walkthrough and warranty.** We do a final inspection, clean the job site and go over your warranty information with you.

### Your roof and the rest of the exterior

A failing roof can let water into the house, damaged gutters, deteriorating siding and extra wear on outdoor living spaces. We are also a deck and gutter contractor, so we look at your whole exterior, not only the shingles, and we check how the roof, the gutters and the roof edge work together. In winter, poor attic ventilation can feed ice dams, which are ridges of ice at the roof edge that push meltwater back under shingles, so the roof and the attic are worth looking at together.

### Roofing questions

**How long does an asphalt shingle roof last?**

Often 20 to 30 years, and sometimes more or less. Ventilation, the weather your roof has seen, how well it was installed and any storm damage all change the number.

**Is metal roofing worth it?**

It depends on your budget and how long you plan to stay. Metal usually costs more at the start and often lasts longer than asphalt, so it can suit a home you plan to keep.

**Does a new roof need a permit?**

Sometimes. The rules depend on your town, so the local permit office has the final word.

**Should I call my insurer or a roofer first?**

Report the storm soon after it happens, because policies often set a deadline for reporting damage. Then call us for an inspection. What gets paid is your company's call. We can document the damage but can't promise coverage.
