### Does the Roof Need Replacing?

Before we talk about a new roof, we find out whether you need one. Not every leak points to a worn-out roof. When one spot has failed, we repair that spot. When the damage is spread across the roof, or the roof is sagging or letting sunlight through the roof boards, it may be time to replace it. Roofs also wear out with age, and the longer you wait, the bigger the problem can get.

Either way, we tell you what we found, and you decide.

### What Usually Fails First

On most asphalt shingle roofs, trouble starts at the weak points before it shows in the open field of shingles. The flashing (metal strips sealed around chimneys and walls), the seals around vent pipes and the valleys where two slopes meet are often the first places water gets in.

Those spots can often be fixed without a new roof.

Water also travels along the roof framing before it drips. A stain on a ceiling is often not under the spot where the water came in, which is why a good inspection looks at the roof and the attic both.

### Repair or Replace

In most cases, a repair makes sense when the problem is in one place and the rest of the roof is sound. Replacement usually makes sense when repairs keep adding up, when the shingles have lost their gritty protective coating across much of the roof, or when the roof is near the end of its expected life.

Asphalt roofs often last somewhere around 20 to 30 years, depending on the shingle, the attic ventilation and how many Nebraska hailstorms they've been through. Some other materials last longer. If you've paid for repairs more than once in a few years, it's worth asking what a new roof would cost instead.

### Before You Call

Please don't climb up to check the roof yourself, because many falls happen on slopes that look easy. From the ground, with binoculars or a zoomed phone photo, you can often spot missing or lifted shingles.

If you look in the attic, step only on the framing, because the ceiling below it won't hold your weight.

### Choosing the Material

We install a range of roofing materials: asphalt, stone coated steel, steel, standing seam steel (metal panels with raised seams), rubber slate (a rubber look-alike for stone slate), cedar shake (wood shingles), polymer, impact resistance options, and more. We come to your property and look at your home before we recommend one, because the right material depends on your house and what you need from it.

### First Steps After a Storm

Stay inside until the storm has passed, then look at the house from the ground. Don't go up on a wet or damaged roof, and stay well away from downed power lines.

If water is coming through a ceiling, move belongings out of the way.

Take dated photos of everything before you move or clean anything. In most cases, covering an opening quickly keeps a small problem from becoming a bigger one.

### One Company for the Whole Repair

We handle the restoration after storm damage: roofing, siding, windows, doors and paint. You can call one company instead of finding a separate contractor for each part of your house.

### What Storms Usually Do

Hail often bruises shingles and knocks the gritty protective coating loose, and from the ground you may see nothing at all. A roof can be damaged long before it leaks. Dented gutters, vents and window trim are often a clue, because thin metal shows hail more easily than shingles do. In Eastern Nebraska, spring and summer storms are when most hail and wind damage happens, so it's worth looking the roof over after every big one, even if nothing leaks.

Wind more often lifts or creases shingles and peels up the caps along the roof peak and the roof vents. The side of the house facing the storm usually takes the worst of it.

Inside, wet insulation and drywall often need to dry out quickly. In many cases mold can start within a couple of days, so don't wait to deal with a wet ceiling just because the roof is covered.

### Should You File a Claim?

Filing a claim can raise your rates, so it matters when you file and when you don't. We come out, assess the damage and tell you whether a claim is worth filing.

Many policies carry a separate wind and hail deductible (the amount you pay before the insurance does), sometimes a percentage of the home's value rather than a flat amount. If the repair costs less than your deductible, a claim may not pay anything. Many policies also set a deadline for reporting storm damage, so read yours early.

### Repair or Replace After a Storm

If wind took a few shingles off an otherwise sound roof, a repair is usually enough. If hail hit most of the slopes, fixing them one at a time is rarely practical, and replacement is often the result.

Keep receipts for emergency work like tarping. Policies often cover reasonable temporary repairs, but check yours. An adjuster (the insurance company's inspector) usually looks at the damage first, so it helps to have your photos and a list of what you found ready for that visit.
