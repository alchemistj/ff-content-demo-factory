### When the system stops

A house that stays far too cold or too hot for long is a health risk for babies, older adults and pets, so call as soon as you know the system isn't keeping up. Tell us what you see and hear: no air, warm air, no heat, a noise, a smell, water near the unit. That description helps us decide what to bring, and it often tells us where to start.

### Check these before you call

Two minutes of checking sometimes ends the problem. Make sure the thermostat is set to heat or cool, with the setting above or below the room temperature, and that its batteries are fresh. Look at your breaker panel for a tripped breaker, and at the switch on or near the furnace, which looks like a light switch and is sometimes turned off by accident. Check the air filter: a filter packed with dust restricts airflow, and restricted airflow can shut a furnace down or freeze the indoor part of an air conditioner. If all of that looks right, leave the panels closed and call us.

### Gas smell and carbon monoxide: safety first

If you smell gas, leave the house and call your gas utility or 911 from outside. Do not flip switches or use a phone inside. If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, get everyone outside and call 911 before you call us. After a snowstorm, check that snow or debris isn't blocking the furnace vent on the outside of your house.

### What usually fails in an air conditioner

If the outdoor unit hums but the fan or compressor (the pump at the heart of the cooling system) doesn't start, a worn capacitor is often the cause. A capacitor is a small part that gives the motors the jolt they need to start. A failed fan motor in the outdoor unit leaves the system running hot and cooling poorly. Weak cooling often points to a refrigerant leak, because an air conditioner doesn't use up refrigerant. Water near the indoor unit is often a clogged drain line, the pipe that carries away the moisture the system pulls from your air, and many systems answer a clogged line by shutting the air conditioner off.

### What usually fails in a furnace

A furnace that lights and shuts off within seconds often has a dirty flame sensor, the small rod that confirms the burner is lit. One that clicks and never lights often has a worn igniter, the part that lights the burner. A blower that won't run, or runs weakly, is often a failed motor. A furnace that overheats and shuts down usually has restricted airflow from a dirty filter or blocked vents. High-efficiency furnaces also drain water, and a clogged drain or an iced-over outside vent pipe can stop them in a hard Lincoln freeze.

A new buzz, rattle, squeal or grinding from the outdoor unit or the furnace is usually a part that is wearing out. Replacing a part before it fails often costs less than the breakdown it would cause, and it can protect the motor or compressor beside it. If you hear a new noise, call before the system stops.

### Keep it from failing again

Check the filter every month and replace it every one to three months, sooner in a home with pets or dust. A medium-efficiency pleated filter suits most homes, because very high-rated filters can strain the blower. Keep supply and return vents clear of rugs and furniture, and keep the outdoor unit clear of leaves, grass and snow, with a couple of feet of open space around it. A tune-up usually covers the parts that fail most: coils, electrical connections, burner and ignition parts, and the vent and drain.

### Repair questions

#### How long does a repair take?

It depends on the problem and the part. Many repairs take under an hour once the cause is found, and a part that has to be ordered takes longer.

#### My air conditioner has ice on it. What should I do?

Turn the cooling off and run only the fan so the ice melts. Ice usually points to restricted airflow, often a dirty filter, or to low refrigerant from a leak. A new filter fixes the first. The second needs a technician.

#### Should I turn the thermostat up when it's extremely cold?

No. A higher setting doesn't heat the house faster, it only keeps the furnace running longer. Turn it down a couple of degrees, add layers and call us if the furnace runs nonstop and the house still won't warm.

### Sizing a system to your house

A new system should fit your house, not just match the old unit's size. A proper sizing looks at your square footage, insulation, windows, ductwork and how much sun the house gets. An air conditioner that is too large often cools the air quickly and shuts off before it removes much humidity, which leaves rooms damp and cool. One that is too small runs nearly nonstop on hot days. Leaky or undersized ducts can hold back even a well-sized system, so they are worth checking before you spend on new equipment.

### Efficiency ratings

Efficiency ratings are printed on every new unit. Air conditioners carry a SEER2 number, which rates how much cooling you get for the electricity used, and furnaces an AFUE percentage, which is how much of the fuel becomes heat. Higher is better in both. Furnaces built decades ago often turn only 60 to 70 percent of their fuel into heat, and new gas furnaces are rated as high as 97 percent. A higher rating costs more up front and pays back over the years, and how fast depends on how much you run the system. Insulation, sealed windows and thermostat habits move your bill too.

### Heat pumps

A heat pump heats and cools with one outdoor unit. Many Nebraska homes keep a gas furnace as backup for the coldest days, and newer models built for cold weather handle low temperatures better than older ones. We replace heat pumps, and we help you work out which setup fits your house.

Heated winter air in Nebraska often runs dry, and a whole-house humidifier adds moisture through your furnace. A whole-house air purifier works on the air that passes through your ducts. We install both with a new furnace or air conditioner, so the system is set up once.

Replacing the furnace and air conditioner together costs more at once, and often costs less than two separate visits. ### Replacement questions

#### Should I replace the furnace and air conditioner together?

If both are old, replacing them together often saves a second visit, and the indoor and outdoor equipment can be matched. If one is newer, replacing only the old one is possible, but the new unit has to work with the equipment already there.

#### Will a new system lower my bills?

It often does when the old system was inefficient, and less when the ducts leak or the house has poor insulation. A whole-home approach, with the right equipment, insulation, sealed windows and sensible thermostat settings, gets the best result.
