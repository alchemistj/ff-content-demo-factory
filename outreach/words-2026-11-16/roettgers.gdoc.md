### What Usually Goes Wrong With an Air Conditioner

Most "AC not cooling" calls come down to a short list of causes. In most cases it's one of these:

  - **A weak or failed capacitor.** It's a small part that gives the outdoor unit's motors a jolt to start, including the compressor, the pump at the heart of the system. Heat and age wear capacitors out, and a bad one often shows up as an outdoor unit that hums but doesn't start. It's usually one of the less costly repairs.
  - **A refrigerant leak.** Refrigerant is the fluid that carries heat out of your house. It isn't used up like fuel, so a system that's low usually has a leak somewhere. Topping it off without finding the leak often means the same problem comes back.
  - **A frozen indoor coil (the part inside that chills the air).** Often caused by restricted airflow, from a dirty filter or a blocked vent, or by low refrigerant. Ice blocks the air, and the system blows warm.
  - **A dirty outdoor coil.** Grass clippings, leaves and cottony seeds can pack the unit and keep it from shedding heat.
  - **A failed contactor (the switch that powers the outdoor unit on) or fan motor.** Both are common wear parts, and both often show up as a unit that clicks, buzzes or runs without the fan turning.
  - **A clogged condensate drain.** Many systems shut themselves off when the drain backs up, which can look like a dead air conditioner.

### Check These Before You Call

Three quick checks sometimes solve it, and they help whoever you call:

  - **Thermostat.** Make sure it's set to cool, set a few degrees below the room temperature, and has fresh batteries if it takes them.
  - **Breaker.** The outdoor unit usually has its own breaker. If it's tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, leave it off and call.
  - **Filter.** A clogged filter is one of the most common causes of weak airflow and frozen coils. If it's dirty, replace it.

Then tell us what you found, along with what the system is doing and when it started.

### Safety Comes Before Cooling

If you see ice on the indoor unit or the copper lines, turn the system off at the thermostat and let it thaw before anyone runs it again. In most cases the fan alone can be left running to speed that up. Don't chip the ice off.

Leave the outdoor unit's electrical panel and the parts behind it alone. Some of them can hold a charge even with the power off. If you smell something burning, hear grinding or loud squealing, or see water around the indoor unit, shut the system off at the thermostat and the breaker. Running a system that's making those noises can turn a small repair into a compressor failure.

During a Lincoln heat wave, close blinds, run fans and watch anyone who is elderly, very young or has health problems. They overheat faster than most people expect.

### What to Expect as a Homeowner

Ask what failed and whether other parts are likely to be next. A new part in a system that's otherwise in good shape is usually a good repair. The same part in a system that has already needed several repairs deserves a harder look.

Older systems may use a refrigerant that's no longer made. That can make a leak repair expensive, and it's one reason an older unit sometimes costs more to keep than to replace. Before you approve a leak repair, ask which refrigerant the system uses and get the price in writing.

### How the Decision Usually Works

A common rule of thumb is to compare the repair bill with the cost of a new system. If the repair is more than about half of that, replacement often makes more sense. Age matters too. A major failure on an older unit, such as the compressor (the pump at the heart of the system) or a large leak of refrigerant, the fluid that carries heat out of the house, often points toward replacement.

A failed small electrical part or fan motor on a system in decent shape is usually a repair. Hail is a Lincoln factor too: it can bend the fins on the outdoor coil, and on an old unit that damage can tip the decision toward replacement.

### A Free Estimate, Then the Choice Is Yours

The estimate is free and comes before any commitment. Once we've looked at your system, you'll know what a new one involves and what it costs before you decide.

### What a Replacement Usually Involves

A proper replacement starts with sizing. A unit that's too big often cools the house in short bursts and does a poor job removing humidity. One that's too small can run all afternoon and still fall behind. Square footage alone isn't enough, so expect questions about your windows, insulation, ductwork and which rooms are hard to cool.

The old refrigerant has to be recovered by a certified technician, not released into the air. The new outdoor unit and indoor coil are usually matched, and the concrete pad, the shutoff box, the copper lines between the indoor and outdoor units and the thermostat get checked at the same time. A straightforward swap can often be done in a day, and ductwork or electrical changes add time.

### Before the Estimate Visit

A few things make the estimate more accurate:

  - The age and model number, from the label on the outdoor unit
  - What's been repaired, and when
  - Which rooms never get cool enough
  - How long you plan to stay in the house

When you compare any estimates, ask what size is being recommended and how it was figured. Ask whether the indoor coil is included and what the warranty covers, in writing. A higher efficiency rating (the SEER number on the unit's label) usually costs more up front. The savings depend on how many hours a year you run the air conditioner, and Lincoln's cooling season is shorter than in southern states, so a long payback is common.
