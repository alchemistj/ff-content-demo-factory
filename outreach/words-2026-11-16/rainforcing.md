## The Four Steps

1. **Free damage inspection.** We photograph the roof with a drone and take close-up photos of hail hits, shingles lifted by wind and any other damage.
2. **Help filing.** We help you file with your insurance company and give you our photo report and findings to send with the claim.
3. **Meeting your adjuster.** We meet that person at your house so you can go over the damage together, including the items a new roof needs to meet your town's building codes.
4. **Rebuild and final paperwork.** We install the new roof, clean up, and send proof that the work is finished to your insurance company. That proof is often what releases the held-back part of a payment.

## Why We Start With a Drone

Hail damage often doesn't show from the ground. Hail can knock off the gritty coating on top of a shingle or crack the layer underneath, and a roof like that can look fine from the yard. A drone lets us photograph every part of the roof, including steep slopes that are risky to walk, and the time-stamped photos give you and your adjuster the same pictures to look at. A roof that has just taken hail can also lose more of that coating under a person's boots, so staying off it until the damage is known is usually safer for the roof and for the person.

## When the Adjuster Offers Only a Patch

Sometimes the first estimate pays for a repair of the damaged spot, and the problem is that your shingle style isn't made anymore. New shingles in a different style may not match or seal the same way, so we document that the style was discontinued and submit the evidence for your insurance company to review.

Code is the other common gap. A new roof often has to include things the old one didn't: a waterproof layer along the eaves and in the valleys where two slopes meet, a metal strip along the roof's edge, and better attic ventilation. If your estimate leaves those out, we cite your local building codes to your insurance company. Many policies include ordinance-or-law coverage, which helps pay for code-required upgrades, so check yours.

## How Long a Claim Can Take, and What a Denial Means

The roofing takes days. The claim can take much longer. Insurance companies often send their own inspector, and if your roofer and that inspector measure the damage differently, a second inspection may follow. A few weeks is common, and some claims run for months.

Insurance companies also sometimes deny a claim or approve less than the roof needs. A denial isn't always the end: better photos, a second inspection or a cited building code can change a decision, though nothing guarantees it. We stay on the claim with you and tell you plainly what we see.

## What Your Policy Decides, and What to Do If Water Is Coming In

Two parts of your policy shape what a claim feels like. The first is the deductible, the part you pay yourself. For wind and hail, some deductibles are a percentage of your home's insured value rather than a flat dollar amount, which can be a surprise.

The second is how the policy pays on a roof. Many pay in two parts: a first payment with the roof's age-related wear subtracted, called depreciation, and the rest after the work is done and proof is sent. That is why dated photos and receipts for any emergency work matter to the file. If water is coming in now, call us: we can put a temporary tarp over the damaged area to keep more water out until the permanent repair, and many policies expect you to protect the house from further damage.

## Does the Roof Need Replacing at All?

Our rule: if the trouble is in one place and the rest of the roof is sound, repair that place. If damage is spread across several slopes, if the roof is past about 15 to 20 years old and leaks in more than one spot, or if repairs keep coming back, price a replacement.

One clue shows up in the gutters and under the downspouts: piles of gritty granules, the coating on top of the shingles. Heavy granule loss usually means the shingles are wearing out, and a roof in that shape is better replaced than patched. A visible dip along the ridge or roof deck is another reason to call, since it can point to rotted boards underneath.

## Where Old Roofs Usually Fail

Leaks more often start where the roof changes direction or something passes through it than in the middle of the shingles: around chimneys, vent pipes and skylights, and in the valleys where two slopes meet. The thin metal and sealant that close those joints, called flashing, wear out before the shingles do.

Water also travels. It often runs along the boards before it drips, so a ceiling stain is usually not directly under the hole. Wood that stays damp for a long time rots, which is why a replacement sometimes includes new boards under the shingles.

## What Peoria Weather Does to a Roof

Central Illinois roofs take freezing and thawing, snow, spring storms and hail. Repeated freezing and thawing loosens old sealant and cracks brittle shingles, and damage from one storm sometimes doesn't turn into a leak until the next winter.

Ice dams are a common winter problem. Heat escapes into the attic, melts the snow on the roof, and the water refreezes at the cold edge. That ice holds back later meltwater, which can back up under the shingles. Insulation and attic ventilation matter as much as the roof itself, so a new roof on an attic that still leaks heat can have the same trouble.

## How a Replacement Goes

1. **Inspection.** We check the roof deck (the boards under the shingles), the ventilation, the flashing and the other parts of the roof's exterior, and we photograph what we find.
2. **Insurance and code check.** If a storm caused the damage, we document it and help with your insurance company's requirements. We also line up the work with local building codes.
3. **Installation.** The crew strips the old roof down to the boards, puts down a waterproof layer where the codes call for one, and installs the new roof.
4. **Cleanup and walk-through.** We clear the debris, sweep the yard for stray nails, and walk the finished roof to check the work.
