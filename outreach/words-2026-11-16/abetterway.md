## Why We Tear Off to Bare Wood

The boards a roof is built on are called the decking. Over the years some of them can soften or rot, usually where water has been getting in. If the old underlayment stays on, nobody can see those boards, and a new roof laid over them can hide the problem. So we strip the roof, look at the wood, and replace damaged boards instead of roofing over them.

Underlayment is the black layer, called felt paper, between the wood and the shingles. We put new felt on our replacements. We haven't been comfortable with the newer synthetic underlayments, so we stay with felt. Reusing old underlayment can save a little money, but we don't think it's worth the risk to the roof.

## Repair or Replace

A repair makes sense when the damage is in one place and the rest of the roof is sound: a few missing shingles, a single leak, one damaged vent. Replacement usually makes more sense when the trouble is spread across the roof, when the same roof keeps needing repairs, or when it has reached the end of its life.

Asphalt shingle roofs often last somewhere around 20 to 25 years, depending on the shingle, how well the attic is ventilated and the weather, and Nebraska supplies plenty of it: summer heat, hail, strong wind and freezing winters. Here is a rule you can check from the yard. Shingles curling or cracking across several slopes, or piles of the colored grit that coats shingles collecting at the bottom of the downspouts, point to a roof wearing out broadly rather than in one spot. Either way, an aged or storm-hit roof doesn't always need replacing, and we'll tell you if a repair will do.

## Ventilation Under the New Roof

A roof can fail from the inside. Hot, moist air that can't leave the attic bakes shingles from below and rots wood. In a Nebraska winter it can also melt the snow on the roof so the water refreezes along the lower edge as an ice dam, which can back water up under the shingles. We specialize in ventilation, and we plan it as part of a replacement.

One thing we watch for is mixing two types of vents on the same section of roof. We generally don't do it, because the vents can end up pulling air from each other instead of drawing fresh air in through the vents under the lower edge of the roof. That leaves most of the attic unvented, and in strong wind it can pull rain or snow in.

## What a Replacement Includes

A new roof is more than shingles. A replacement from us includes:

- New metal flashing, the thin metal that seals where the roof meets a wall, chimney or vent, in colors that match your shingles or trim, instead of reusing old rusty metal
- A self-sealing waterproof membrane, often sold as ice and water shield, along the lower edge and in the valleys (where two roof slopes meet), which often helps stop meltwater from backing up under shingles and which many cities' building codes now call for
- Metal edging along the lower edge and the sides that guides water off the roof and away from the wood trim
- New rubber collars around plumbing and exhaust pipes, and paint on old metal vent pipes so they don't stand out against the new shingles
- Shingles from a reputable distributor, backed by a company representative if there is ever a concern about the product
- Cleanup and hauling away of the old roofing

Some flashing tucked under your siding may need to be reused, because we can't remove the siding to replace it.

## Shingles and Wind

Shingles carry wind ratings, from 60 miles per hour up to 150, tested in wind tunnels. A house in the middle of town, sheltered by brick walls and trees, rarely sees what a house on an open hill does, so a higher rating matters more out in the open. We'll go over the options with you.

How shingles are fastened counts too. We nail instead of staple, using the right nails driven into solid decking, and more nails for a higher wind rating. Dust can coat the sealing strips on new shingles, and cold can keep them from sealing, which is one more reason the timing of the job matters.

## Winter Replacements

Some roofs can't wait for spring. We can replace a roof in the cold months, and there can be upsides: crews are often easier to schedule, and shingles are less floppy to handle when it isn't hot. The trade-offs are real, though. Shingles seal down with the help of warmth, so a roof put on in freezing weather may not seal fully until the days warm up, and ice and snow slow the work and make it riskier for the crew.

As a rule of thumb, roofing goes best above freezing, and the shingle maker's instructions give the limits. If your roof is leaking in January, a temporary patch can sometimes hold until spring. We'll tell you what yours needs.

## Start From the Ground

Please stay off the roof. A roof that has been hit by hail or wind can be slick, steep and weaker than it looks, and your weight can crack shingles that are already damaged. Some storm damage can be seen from the ground and some can't, so we recommend having a professional look at all of it.

From the yard, check the gutters, downspouts, vents and siding first. Dents and dings there often mean hail hit the roof too, and so do small piles of shingle grit at the bottom of the downspouts. Wind is different: it tends to lift or crease shingles, or tear some off. Take dated photos of everything you see before anything is cleaned up or moved.

## How a Claim Usually Goes

Every policy and every insurer is different, so this is the usual shape, not a promise. You report the damage. The insurance company often sends an adjuster, the person who inspects the roof for the insurer, and writes an estimate of what it will pay. You pay a deductible, the amount you cover before the insurance pays, and some policies carry a separate, higher deductible for wind and hail, sometimes a percentage of the home's value. Check yours.

Many policies pay either what a new roof would cost or the roof's value today after age and wear, and on an older roof the difference can be large. Read your policy for the deadline to report a claim, since there usually is one. Some insurers may record a claim on your file even when no damage is found, so ask your agent before you file.
