### How a repair visit goes

We start with what you have, because furnaces, boilers and heat pumps each fail in their own ways. Then we pin down the problem: no heat at all, weak heat, a noise or a leak. ### Before you call

If the furnace won't start, three checks take a few minutes. Set the thermostat to heat, a few degrees above the room temperature, and replace the batteries if it has them. ### Gas smell and carbon monoxide

If you smell gas, don't touch a switch, lights included. Go outside, then call your gas utility or 911 from there.

Gas and oil furnaces can produce carbon monoxide when something is wrong. You can't smell it, and the only warning is an alarm. If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, get everyone out of the house and call 911 first. Call us once the house is cleared.

### What usually fails

On gas furnaces, a handful of parts cause most no-heat calls:

- **The igniter,** which lights the burners, can wear out.
- **The flame sensor,** a thin rod that confirms the flame, can get coated so the burners shut off after a few seconds.
- **The inducer motor,** a small fan that clears exhaust, and the **blower motor,** which pushes warm air through the ducts, can fail.
- **The control board,** the circuit board that runs the whole sequence, can fail. Many furnaces flash a numbered code on it, and the number usually points toward the part that failed. The meaning of a code differs by brand.
- **The limit switch,** a safety switch that shuts the burners off when the furnace overheats, often trips because of a clogged filter.

Oil furnaces often stop for a different reason, a clogged nozzle or fuel filter, which is why they usually need cleaning every year.

### Repair or replace?

Age alone doesn't decide it. A furnace commonly lasts 15 to 20 years, and an old one can still be worth repairing when what failed is an igniter, a motor or a control board. A rule of thumb is that when a repair costs about half the price of a new furnace and the furnace is already near the end of its life, replacing it makes more sense. Repeated repairs in one season point the same way.

One failure usually ends the discussion, a cracked heat exchanger. That is the metal chamber where the burner's heat passes to the air your house breathes, and a crack can let combustion gases mix with it. A technician who finds one will usually recommend replacement, and you should not run the furnace until it is addressed.

### Heat pumps and boilers

A heat pump heats by moving warmth from outdoors in, and in freezing weather it periodically melts frost off its outdoor coil, so some steam or dripping is often normal. In the coldest weather many heat pumps switch on electric backup heat, which costs more to run and is worth asking about if your electric bill jumps. A heat pump that blows cold air for more than a few minutes, or an outdoor unit buried in ice, is worth a call.

A hot-water boiler fails differently. Low water pressure on its gauge, air trapped in the pipes or a worn circulator pump (the pump that sends hot water around the house) are common reasons radiators go cold. If the pressure gauge reads near zero, call before adding water yourself.

### When the air conditioner stops cooling in a Fort Wayne summer

Indiana summers bring humid heat, and an air conditioner is also pulling moisture out of the air. When it stops, a house often turns clammy before it turns hot, and a long July run of 90-degree days is when a weak part usually gives out. We handle air conditioning emergency repair. Call and tell us what the system is doing: whether the outdoor unit is running, whether the air from the vents is cool or warm, and whether anything is dripping or frozen.

### Before you call

Three checks sometimes fix it. Set the thermostat to cool, a few degrees below the room temperature. Look at the breaker panel for a tripped switch. Then pull the filter and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it, because a packed filter can freeze the indoor coil and stop the cooling.

Ice is worth knowing about. If you see ice on the indoor coil or on the thicker copper line, turn the system off and let it thaw for several hours with the fan running if you can. Do not chip it off. Turning the system back on over ice usually brings the ice right back.

Outside, check that the unit has room to breathe. Cottonwood fluff, grass clippings and leaves packed against the coil can make an air conditioner run long and cool poorly, and clearing the area by hand is safe once the power is off.

Water matters too. An air conditioner collects water from the air and sends it down a drain line. In many Fort Wayne homes the indoor unit is in the basement, where a small pump may lift the water out. If the drain clogs, a safety switch often shuts the system off, or water can overflow and damage the floor or ceiling below. That is what the water leak detection devices we install are for. A sensor near the unit's drain pan can alert you, or in some setups shut the system down, before the damage spreads.

### Two safety rules

If you reset a breaker and it trips again, leave it off and call us. In most cases that points to a real electrical fault. And please don't open the outdoor unit's panels. Its capacitors, small parts that store a charge, can hold power after the switch is off.

### What usually fails

Most no-cooling calls come down to a short list:

- **A weak capacitor.** It gives the compressor and fan motor their starting jolt, and when it weakens the fan may hum without turning.
- **A worn contactor.** This is the electrical switch that sends power to the outdoor unit, and the unit may click or chatter without starting.
- **A failed fan motor.** The outdoor fan moves the heat off the coil, and the compressor shuts off to protect itself without it.
- **A clogged drain line.** As described above.
- **A dirty coil.** The system usually runs a long time and cools poorly.
- **A refrigerant leak.** The refrigerant is the fluid that carries heat out of your house, and a system that has run low has leaked somewhere.

A failed compressor is the expensive one. It is the pump that moves the refrigerant, and when it goes the repair bill usually decides whether to replace the system.

### Refrigerant, and the older R-22

An air conditioner doesn't use up its refrigerant, so a system that needs more has a leak, and adding refrigerant without finding the leak usually means doing it again. Handling refrigerant also takes a federal EPA certification, so topping it off is not a do-it-yourself job.

Systems built before about 2010 often use R-22, an older refrigerant that many people still call Freon. R-22 production ended in 2020, so what remains is recycled supply that tends to cost more, and a leak repair on one of these can take a large share of what a new system costs. Newer systems use different refrigerants. Which one is in yours is printed on the outdoor unit's label.

### Heat pumps and ductless systems

A heat pump is the same outdoor unit as an air conditioner, with a valve that reverses the flow, so most of the list above applies to it in summer. A ductless mini-split cools one room or one zone from a small indoor unit on the wall. When it drips water inside or cools weakly, the usual causes are a dirty filter or a clogged drain line. We repair ductless systems and heat pumps as well as central air.

### Mobile manufactured homes

We service mobile manufactured homes. These homes often use a combined electric furnace and air handler with ducts running under the floor, and a torn duct or a loose connection in a crawl space can send cooled air outside the house. We also size a replacement system to the home, because a unit that is too large for a small house cools it in short bursts and leaves the air damp.

### Repair or replace?

An air conditioner often lasts 12 to 15 years. A capacitor, contactor or fan motor is usually worth repairing at any age. A failed compressor or a leaking coil on an older unit is harder to justify, and R-22 makes it harder still. Many technicians suggest that a repair above half the price of a new system, on a unit past its midlife, leans toward replacing. A new outdoor unit usually has to match the indoor coil inside the house, so replacing only the outside half is rarely the cheaper answer.
