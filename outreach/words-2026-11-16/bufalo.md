## Why Northern Illinois Roofs Wear Out

Roofs here go through freezing and thawing, ice dams, high winds and summer heat. Repeated freezing loosens old sealant and cracks brittle shingles. Wind lifts the corners and ridge first. Heat dries out the sealant around chimneys and vents, and those joints, more often than the open field of shingles, are where water gets in first.

Water also travels. It often runs along the rafters before it drips, so a ceiling stain is usually not directly under the hole. That's why a roof is worth checking as a whole, not only at the spot you point to. Wood that stays damp for a long time rots, and rotted wood is why a replacement sometimes includes new boards under the shingles.

## What a Replacement Includes

A full replacement from us covers:

- All the old shingles and the waterproof layer beneath them removed down to the wood
- The wood checked for rot, water damage and soft spots, and repaired where needed
- A new waterproof layer over the wood

- New shingles or a metal roof
- A look at attic ventilation, since poor airflow can cause moisture and heat problems

## Asphalt Shingles or Metal

We install both. Asphalt shingles cost less up front, come in many colors, and are common in Rockford for good reason: they handle freeze and thaw, wind and hail well. Metal sheds snow and ice instead of trapping it, resists high winds, reflects summer heat, and often lasts decades longer than shingles, at a higher price. Metal suits steep roofs and owners who don't want to think about the roof again for a long time. Which one fits depends on your house, your budget and how long you plan to stay.

## What Hail and Wind Do to a Roof

Hail can crack shingles, knock off the gritty top layer that shields them from sun, bruise them, and dent metal parts such as vents and gutters. Wind more often lifts shingles at the corners and along the ridge first, which exposes the wood underneath to rain. Either one can leave a roof that looks fine from the yard while its protection is already weaker.

Damage varies from storm to storm. Hail may hit one side of a roof harder than the other, depending on the wind direction, and a lifted shingle that lies back down can hide a crease. That's why a close look on the roof is worth having even when nothing is leaking yet.

## The First Hours After a Storm

Stay off the roof. A wet, steep or damaged roof is dangerous to walk on, and your weight can harm shingles that are already weakened. Look from the ground instead, and walk the yard for shingle pieces, dented gutters or downspouts, and debris.

Inside, check the ceilings and the attic if you can reach it safely. If water is coming in, move your belongings away from it. Most policies expect you to limit further damage where you can. Photograph what you see, with the date, before anything is cleaned up or moved. Then call us and call your insurance company's claim line, and check your policy for the deadline to report, since there usually is one.

We're open 24 hours, and we offer emergency storm service, including temporary repairs, to stop water while the full repair waits for approval.

## How a Claim Usually Goes

Every policy and every insurer is different, so this is the usual shape, not a promise. You report the damage. The insurance company often sends an adjuster, the person who looks at the damage and writes up what the company will pay. You then owe a deductible, the share of the loss your policy leaves to you. The payment may come in more than one step.

Many policies pay either the replacement cost of the roof or its actual cash value, which is lower on an older roof because it subtracts for age and wear. The adjuster's estimate and a contractor's estimate don't always match. When they don't, ask for the reasons in writing.
