## When a Repair Won't Do Anymore

A repair fits when the trouble is in one place and the rest of the roof is sound: a few missing shingles, one leak, one worn spot.

  

Replacement is the right call when the damage or wear is spread across the roof, when the same roof keeps needing repairs, or when a storm has damaged most of it. A close look at the roof, not a guess from the street, settles which one yours is.

  

Age counts too. Asphalt shingle roofs often last somewhere around 20 to 25 years, depending on the shingle, the attic ventilation and the weather. An older roof that leaks in more than one place usually costs more to keep patching than to replace. A newer roof with one damaged spot is usually a repair.

## Where Old Roofs Usually Fail

The flat field of shingles is rarely the first thing to give out. Water more often gets in where the roof changes direction or something sticks through it: around chimneys, vent pipes and skylights, and in the valleys where two slopes meet. The flashing, the metal strips that seal those joints, and the sealant wear out before the shingles do.

  

Water also travels. It often runs along the roof framing or the waterproof layer under the shingles before it drips, so a ceiling stain is usually not directly under the hole. That's why the whole roof and the attic are worth a look, not only the spot you point to. Wood that stays damp for a long time rots, and rotted wood is why a replacement sometimes includes new roof boards, the wooden deck the shingles are nailed to.

## Peoria Weather and Your Roof

Peoria roofs deal with seasonal temperature swings, snow accumulation and storm exposure. We know regional conditions like ice dams and storm damage.

  

An ice dam usually forms when heat escapes from the house into the attic, melts the snow on the roof, and the water refreezes at the cold edge. The ice then holds back meltwater, and that water can back up under the shingles. In most cases the cause is as much the insulation and attic ventilation as the roof itself, so a new roof on an attic that still leaks heat can have the same trouble.

  

Repeated freezing and thawing also loosens old sealant and cracks brittle shingles. Hail and wind add to it, and damage from one storm sometimes doesn't show up as a leak until the following winter.

## After the Hail

A hailstorm can damage more than the shingles.

  

Storms rarely hit just one part of a house.

  

Hail damage is often hard to see from the ground. Hail can knock the gritty protective coating off shingles, bruise them or crack them, and wind can lift and crease shingles without tearing them off. The roof can look fine from the yard while its protection is already weaker. That's one reason a close look is worth having even when nothing is leaking yet.

## First Steps After a Storm

Please stay off the roof. A wet, steep or damaged roof is dangerous to walk on, and your weight can harm shingles that are already weakened. Look from the ground instead, and walk the yard for shingle pieces, dented gutters or downspouts, and debris.

  

Inside, check the ceilings and the attic if you can reach it safely. If water is coming in, a bucket and a move of your belongings away from it can limit the damage. Most policies expect you to prevent further damage where you can.

  

Photograph what you see, with the date, before anything gets cleaned up or moved. Then call us, and call your insurance company's claim line. Check your policy for the deadline to report a claim, since in most cases there is one.

## How a Claim Usually Goes

Every policy and every insurer is different, so what follows is the usual shape and promises nothing. You report the damage. The insurance company often sends an adjuster, its own inspector, to look at the roof and write an estimate of what it will cover. You then pay your deductible, the share the policy leaves to you, and the settlement may be paid in more than one step.

  

Many policies pay either the replacement cost, or the actual cash value, which is the cost minus a deduction for the roof's age. The difference can be large on an older roof. The adjuster's estimate and a contractor's estimate don't always match. When they don't, ask questions and ask for the reasons in writing. We can't promise an outcome.
