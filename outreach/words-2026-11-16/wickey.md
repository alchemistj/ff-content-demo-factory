### What usually goes wrong with a water heater

Tank water heaters tend to fail in a few ways. Electric models have heating elements and thermostats that wear out, which often leaves you with no hot water or only lukewarm water. Gas models depend on a burner, a pilot light or electronic igniter, a safety sensor that shuts off the gas if the flame goes out, and a gas control valve, and any of these can fail. Newer models also carry a circuit board. One of our Leo-Cedarville jobs came down to a bad board and a cut wire, and we ordered a replacement part to finish the repair.

Water coming from the tank itself, not from a pipe fitting on top, usually means the steel has corroded through. That is generally a replacement, not a repair, because the tank can't be patched from the inside.

### What hard water does to a heater

Water across Northeast Indiana is often hard, meaning it carries dissolved minerals such as calcium. Heating the water makes those minerals come out as scale, a crusty deposit. In a tank heater, scale and sediment settle on the bottom, where they can cause popping or rumbling sounds, make the burner or element work harder, and shorten the tank's life. In a tankless heater, scale can build up inside the narrow passages that heat the water, and the unit usually needs periodic cleaning to keep its flow and efficiency.

A water softener, which we also install, treats the water before it reaches the heater. Whether you need one depends on how hard your water is, and a water test will tell you.

### Repair or replace

Age is the first thing to look at, and it is usually printed on the label. Tank heaters often last somewhere around 8 to 12 years, though hard water and skipped maintenance can shorten that. A younger heater with one failed part is often worth repairing. An older heater that needs a part, or any tank that leaks, is often better replaced, since another failure tends to follow. A common rule of thumb is to replace when the repair would cost about half of a new heater. These are guides, not promises, and the right answer depends on your heater.

### Tank or tankless

A tank heater stores hot water, usually 40 or 50 gallons in the homes we have worked in around Fort Wayne, and reheats it as you use it. A tankless heater warms water only when a tap is open, so it doesn't run out in the same way and takes up less room. The limit is how much hot water it can deliver at once, so a shower, a dishwasher and a washing machine running together can be too much for a smaller unit. It usually costs more to install, and a gas tankless unit often needs a larger gas line and different venting than the heater it replaces.

Some tank heaters, called power-vent models, use a fan to push exhaust out through plastic pipe in a wall instead of up a chimney. That gives more choice about where the heater can sit, but the fan needs electricity. We have installed power-vent heaters in Fort Wayne. We walk you through the pros and cons of tank and tankless so you can choose for your home and budget.

### What goes in with a new heater

A replacement is more than swapping the tank. The old unit comes out, the water lines, gas or electric connection and shutoff valves are connected to the new one, and the system is tested before we leave. Some jobs call for extras.

An expansion tank is a small tank on the cold water line that absorbs the extra volume of water as it heats. In a house with a one-way valve or pressure regulator on the incoming water line, that extra water has nowhere to go, and pressure can rise and strain the heater and the fixtures. We have installed expansion tanks on Fort Wayne water heaters, and many homes need one, so it is worth asking.

Cold weather matters for heaters in unheated garages. On one Fort Wayne job we installed a 40-gallon electric heater with an expansion tank in a garage, insulated the water lines and added heat tape, an electric cable that warms a pipe in freezing weather.

### Gas, safety and the settings that matter

Gas heaters need to vent their exhaust, which can contain carbon monoxide, so a carbon monoxide alarm near the heater is widely recommended. Gas line repair for water heaters is part of our work.

Every tank heater has a temperature and pressure relief valve, a safety valve with a pipe running down the side of the tank. If it drips or discharges, call us, and do not cap it. A thermostat setting of about 120 degrees Fahrenheit is commonly recommended to lower the risk of scalds.

### One drain or the whole house

The first question is how many drains are affected. One slow sink usually means a clog in that fixture's own pipe, close to the drain. Several fixtures slowing down at once, or a toilet that gurgles when the washing machine drains, usually points to the main line, the large pipe that carries all of the house's waste out toward the street. In a house with a basement, the lowest drain, often a floor drain or a basement shower, tends to back up first.

Many houses have a cleanout, a capped pipe in the basement or the yard that gives a plumber direct access to the main line. Knowing where yours is helps, and it is worth mentioning when you call.

### Why older Fort Wayne sewer lines fail

Many Fort Wayne neighborhoods date from well before plastic pipe. Homes from before roughly the 1970s often have sewer lines made of clay tile, cast iron or a pressed-fiber pipe that softens with age. Clay tile comes in short sections with a joint every few feet. Cast iron rusts and builds up rough deposits inside. Pressed-fiber pipe can flatten or deform. Shifting soil, frost and the weight of the ground above can push joints out of line or leave a low spot in the pipe.

That low spot is often called a dip or a belly. Water and waste collect in it, paper and solids settle, and the line clogs again and again even after it has been cleared. Clearing the clog doesn't change the dip, so a line like that usually needs a section replaced to stop the repeats.

### Tree roots

Roots grow toward water, and a sewer line leaks a little at its joints, particularly older clay tile and cast iron. A hair-thin root can get in at a joint and grow into a mass that catches paper and waste. Older neighborhoods with mature trees are where this is most common, and fast-growing trees such as maples and willows are often blamed.

The usual signs are slow drains that return every few months, or a tub that gurgles when a toilet is flushed. Roots are normally cut out with a cutting head on a drain cable, which clears the line for a while. If the pipe is cracked or a joint is open, the roots tend to grow back, and the lasting fix is repairing or replacing that stretch of pipe.

### What a camera shows, and what it can't

A drain camera is a small camera on a flexible cable that is pushed down the line to show the pipe from the inside. It can reveal roots, a dip, a crack, a joint that has slipped, or a section that has collapsed. That helps decide between clearing the line, repairing a short section and replacing more of it.

A camera has limits. A bad bend, a collapsed section or heavy buildup can stop it before it reaches the problem. On a slow main line in Fort Wayne, the condition of the pipe kept our camera from reaching the cause. We explored the options, and the customer chose to hold off for now. Even then, the visit usually shows something about the line and what the options are.

### When a pipe has to be replaced

A short repair is often less costly than replacing the whole line, but if the pipe is in poor shape throughout, repeated spot repairs can add up. We have done both. A pipe under a concrete floor is harder to reach. In one New Haven home a collapsed pipe beneath the concrete floor was causing serious drainage problems. We broke up the concrete, dug out the damaged pipe and replaced it, then used a camera to confirm there were no other problems. We backfilled the hole, poured new concrete and reset the toilet once the new floor was in.

### Clogs with a reason behind them

Some clogs come from what goes down the drain. Wipes sold as flushable often don't break up the way toilet paper does, and store-bought chemical drain cleaners often don't dissolve roots or grease, can be hard on older pipes, and can burn whoever opens the pipe next, so mention it if you used one.

Water softeners are another source. A softener cleans itself by flushing water out through a drain line, and if that line is partly blocked, the water can back up onto the floor.

### Backups in heavy rain

A basement drain that backs up in a heavy storm is not always a problem with your own pipe. Some older parts of Fort Wayne are served by combined sewers, which carry storm water and household waste in the same pipe, and a surge in the street main can push water back toward a house. A backwater valve, a one-way valve in the sewer line that stops flow from coming back in, is one option that is sometimes used. Whether it suits a house depends on the line and the basement, and the city and the homeowner usually look after different sections of the pipe, so it helps to know where your line connects.
