### What your main panel does

The main service panel is the metal box, usually in a basement, garage or utility room, where power from the utility meter is split into the circuits that run through your house. The main breaker shuts off everything at once, and each smaller breaker protects one circuit. The panel's size is measured in amps, which is how much electricity can flow through it at one time.

### Why older Fort Wayne homes outgrow their panels

Many homes in Fort Wayne's older neighborhoods were wired when a household ran a few lights, a radio and a refrigerator. Those homes often have 60 amp or 100 amp service, and some still have a fuse box. Since then, most added central air, an electric dryer, a dishwasher, a finished basement and sometimes a hot tub or an EV charger. A panel that was fine in 1955 can be asked to carry far more than it was built for, and the result is often breakers that trip or new circuits squeezed into a box with no room.

A 200 amp service is common in newer homes, and it is often what an electrician suggests when a house runs several large appliances. Whether you need it depends on what you run. Before recommending a size, an electrician adds up your loads in what is called a load calculation. Some houses only need a panel with more open slots, not a bigger service.

### When a panel needs replacing, not another breaker

Age alone doesn't decide it. A well-kept panel can last decades, so condition and capacity matter more than the date. A fuse box is a common reason to replace. It's easy for a blown fuse to be swapped for a larger one than the wiring can safely carry, and some insurance companies ask about fuse boxes, so check with yours.

A panel with no open slots is another, since doubling up breakers to squeeze in circuits is a workaround, not a fix. Rust, water stains or scorch marks inside, usually from a damp basement or a loose connection, point to replacement too. Some panels made decades ago have a record of breakers that fail to trip when they should, and an electrician can tell you whether yours is one of them.

A warm cover, a buzzing sound or a burning smell is a reason to call, not to open the panel. The inside carries live power even with the main breaker off, which is why this is work for a licensed electrician and not a weekend project.

### What a 100 to 200 amp upgrade usually involves

The work often covers more than the panel. A new panel and breakers go in, along with heavier wire from the meter to the panel, called the service cable. The meter base and riser are often replaced at the same time, and the grounding is brought up to current code, meaning the connection that gives stray current a safe path into the earth, usually through metal rods driven into the ground or the water line.

Most panel changes need a permit and an inspection by the local building department. Your electric utility, in Fort Wayne usually Indiana Michigan Power, typically disconnects power at the meter and reconnects it afterward, and the power is usually off for several hours, sometimes much of a day. If you have a sump pump, a freezer or medical equipment on a plug, mention it when you book, since they will be off for part of that time.

Code also calls for clear working space in front of a panel, often about three feet. Closets, shelving and finished basement walls built around an old panel sometimes mean the panel has to move, which adds work to the job.

### Meter base and riser

The meter base is the box on the outside of the house that the utility meter plugs into. The riser is the pipe that carries wires up from the meter to where the utility's lines connect, usually near the roof. Rust, storm damage or a riser pulled loose by a falling branch are common reasons to replace them. The utility generally owns the meter and its own wires, so the part on the house side is the electrician's, and the two sides are scheduled together.

### Surge protection, generators and EV chargers

These all connect at the panel. A whole-home surge protector is usually fitted there, and a generator's transfer switch connects through it. An EV charger often needs its own dedicated 40 or 50 amp circuit, and a hot tub or swim spa can need something similar. A crowded panel is often what limits those projects, so we look at it first.

Panel pricing differs from house to house, and the details that move it are things a look at your house settles. They include the size of the upgrade, whether the service cable, meter base and riser are replaced, how far the panel is from the meter, whether it has to move, and the permit. Every one is worth asking about, since each can change the number.

### When a breaker keeps tripping

A breaker trips to stop too much current in a circuit before the wires overheat, so a trip is the breaker doing its job. The usual causes are too many things on one circuit, a short circuit (a hot wire touching another wire or metal), a ground fault (current leaking where it shouldn't, often from water) or a loose connection. Resetting it again and again doesn't fix any of them, and it hides the one clue that tells an electrician where to look.

Breakers also wear out. One that trips with almost nothing running, won't reset, or feels hot is sometimes the problem itself, and replacing it is a small job. Breakers are made for specific panels, and a wrong or poorly fitted one can fail to trip when it should, which is why this isn't a swap to do yourself.

### Outlets that quit

A dead outlet is often fed through another outlet somewhere else. Ground fault outlets, called GFCIs, are in kitchens, bathrooms, garages and outdoors, and they cut power to everything downstream of them when they trip. Tracing which one controls a dead outlet takes a tester and patience, and the fix is sometimes a single reset or a worn outlet.

An outlet that is warm, discolored or buzzing is a different matter. Heat usually means a loose connection or overloaded wiring, and it can be an early step toward a fire. Do not keep using it.

### What old wiring in Fort Wayne often looks like

Fort Wayne has many houses built before 1960, especially in the neighborhoods around downtown. What is behind their walls depends on the decade. Before about 1940 you may find knob-and-tube wiring, where single wires run through the walls on porcelain supports, and cloth-covered wire whose insulation can crumble with age. Outlets with two slots and no ground hole often mean the circuit has no ground wire at all. Some homes from the 1960s and early 1970s have aluminum wiring, which expands and loosens at connections more than copper does, and an electrician can tell you whether yours does.

Old wiring also has fewer circuits. When one circuit feeds a whole floor, a hair dryer and a space heater are enough to trip it. Knob-and-tube wiring is not designed to be buried in insulation, and that is worth knowing before adding it to an attic.

Not all old wiring is dangerous. Much of it is fine until something is added, a wall is opened or a panel is changed, and an electrician looks at what is actually there. One house can have three kinds of wiring from three eras.

### Whole-home rewire: what it means and when it makes sense

A whole-home rewire replaces the wires that run from the panel to every outlet, switch and light, usually with a new panel. It is a bigger job than a repair, and it makes sense when the problem is the wiring throughout, and not a single bad circuit. Examples are ungrounded circuits through the whole house, cloth insulation that has crumbled, aluminum connections that keep failing, or the same trouble showing up in room after room.

A rewire can be done in stages, a few circuits at a time, so the house stays usable. If the problem is in a few circuits, replacing only those is often the better answer. A remodel or addition is a good time, because the walls are already open and new wire costs less to run. In older plaster walls built over wood strips, an electrician often runs new wire through attics, basements and crawl spaces, and opens small sections of wall, which are patched afterward.

Current code requires ground fault and arc fault protection in many rooms. An arc fault device shuts a circuit off when it detects the kind of damaged wire or loose connection that can spark. Adding them is a common reason an older home benefits from new wiring.
