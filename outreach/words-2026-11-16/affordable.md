### When a Repair Is Enough, and When It Isn't

A repair usually makes sense when the trouble is in one place and the rest of the roof is in good shape: a few lifted shingles, one failed flashing, one leak you can trace. A replacement usually makes more sense when damage or wear is spread across a slope, when the same roof keeps needing repairs, or when the roof is near the end of its age range.

Asphalt shingle roofs often last somewhere around 20 to 25 years, depending on the shingle, the attic ventilation and the weather. A newer roof with one damaged spot is usually a repair. An older roof that leaks in more than one place usually costs more to keep patching than to replace. When we come out, we tell you which one yours is.

### Where Water Usually Gets In

The open field of shingles is rarely the first place a roof gives out. Water more often gets in where the roof changes direction or something passes through it: around chimneys, plumbing vents and skylights, and in the valleys where two slopes meet. The metal flashing and sealant in those spots often wear out before the shingles do.

Water can also travel along the decking or a rafter before it drips, so a ceiling stain is often not directly under the hole. If you suspect a leak, please stay off the roof, since a steep or wet roof is dangerous to walk on. Look from the ground, check the ceiling and the attic if you can reach them safely, and take dated photos before you call.

### Ice, Heat and What Goes Under the Shingles

Snow that melts and refreezes at the eaves can form an ice dam, and the ice can push meltwater back up under the shingles. Heat escaping from the house into the attic is a common cause, so insulation and ventilation count as much as the shingles. Ice and water barrier along the eaves is there for this reason.

The written quote lists the layers you can't see once the roof is done: underlayment and ice and water barrier, ventilation, flashing and drip edge, and starter and ridge shingles. If the house needs ridge vent or other ventilation updates, that is part of the job and part of the price.

Boards or sheets that are soft or damaged are replaced before the new roof goes on. Sometimes that's a few sheets, and sometimes it's the whole deck. We tell you up front how decking is handled on your quote.

If there are two layers of old shingles instead of one, there is more labor and more disposal.

### Replacing a Roof in Winter

We don't do a lot of winter roofs, but when a homeowner needs one done, we do it. Cold changes how shingles go on, not whether they can. Asphalt shingles are stiffer below about 40°F, and the sealant strip on each shingle needs the sun's heat to bond, so shingles installed in the cold may not seal until warmer, sunny days.

Owens Corning's cold-weather steps include hand-sealing the shingles around the edge of the roof, so wind can't lift them before the strip bonds. We don't treat that step as optional.

### Asphalt Shingles in Plain Words

Most shingle roofs on homes today are one of two kinds. A 3-tab shingle is flat, a single layer with evenly spaced cutouts. An architectural shingle, also called laminated or dimensional, is thicker and layered, with shadow lines and no uniform tabs. Architectural shingles are the common choice now, and most of the roofs we install are architectural.

The 3-tab type usually wears out sooner, often around 20 years under normal conditions. Architectural shingles often last about 25 years in northeast Indiana, based on what we see on Fort Wayne roofs. Both numbers are guidelines. Installation, attic ventilation and weather move them up or down.

### Duration or Oakridge

Owens Corning Duration is the shingle we install most, and we install Oakridge and many other brands as well. Both are architectural shingles, and from the street they look much alike.

The difference is where the nails go. Duration has SureNail Technology, a woven fabric strip across the nailing area. Owens Corning lists Duration at a 130-mph wind warranty with the standard four nails. Oakridge has no fabric strip. Owens Corning lists it at 110 mph with four nails, and at 130 mph only with six nails and Owens Corning starter shingles along the eaves and rakes.

### Which One Fits Your House

Wind exposure is the first question. A house on an open lot, by a lake or at the edge of a farm field takes more wind than one tucked into an older Fort Wayne neighborhood, and on those roofs we lean toward Duration. A ranch or Cape Cod with one or two main roof planes looks sharp in Oakridge, and Oakridge is the one to look at if you want a dependable roof on a tighter budget.

Color can decide it. Some colors come only in one line, so pick the color first and then check which line has it. We quote both lines side by side, so you see the difference on your own roof.

### How the Shingles Are Nailed

Shingles are held down by nails in a marked nailing area, and manufacturers usually require nails in that area for the warranty to apply. Nails placed too high or too low can leave a shingle easier for wind to lift. That is why the nail count and the starter shingles at the eaves and rakes matter as much as the name on the bundle.

Before you sign with any roofer, ask how many nails per shingle, whether starter shingles go at the eaves and rakes, and what goes under the shingles. The roof also needs a sound deck, the right underlayment and ventilation under it. We put our attention there on every tear-off.

### What Wears an Asphalt Roof Out Early

A hot attic can bake shingles from below, and poor ventilation often traps that heat and moisture. South- and west-facing slopes usually wear first, since they take the most afternoon sun. Freezing and thawing loosens brittle old sealant, and hail and wind add to the wear. Damage from one storm may not show up as a leak until the next winter.

Dark streaks on a roof are often algae. Both Duration and Oakridge come with StreakGuard algae protection, and the length of that protection varies by product and region.

### Warranty Is Not Lifespan

Both lines carry the Owens Corning Limited Lifetime shingle warranty, for as long as you own the home. The warranty document spells out the coverage, limitations and requirements, and we go over it with you.

A "lifetime" label describes the manufacturer's limited warranty on the shingles. It doesn't promise how long the roof will last on your house, and it doesn't stop wind, hail, ice dams or a poorly vented attic from wearing a roof out early.
