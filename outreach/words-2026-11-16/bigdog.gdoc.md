When Replacement Makes More Sense Than Another Repair
A repair is usually the right call when the damage is in one place and the roof still has useful life. Replacement starts to make more sense when repairs keep coming, the shingles are brittle across several slopes, or the roof is near the end of its life.

Age matters. Asphalt roofs often last somewhere between 20 and 30 years, depending on the shingle, the attic ventilation and the weather. In Fort Wayne, wind, hail, humidity and freeze-thaw cycles speed up granule loss and seal failure, so a roof past about 15 years usually deserves a closer look. Water stains in different rooms, repeated blow-offs and flashing failures in several places often point to the whole roof rather than one spot.
Where Old Roofs Usually Fail
The flat run of shingles is rarely the first thing to give out. Water more often gets in where the roof meets something: a chimney, a sidewall, a skylight, a valley, or a pipe sticking through. The metal flashing and sealant at those spots wear out before the shingles do.

Rubber pipe boots are a common example. The rubber often cracks within about 10 years, and the leak shows up as a slow drip near a bathroom or kitchen vent stack. When we replace pipe boots, we prefer lead-wrapped boots, which usually outlast the shingles.

Water also travels. It can run along the rafters or the underlayment before it drips, so a ceiling stain is often not under the hole. Wood that stays damp for a long time rots, and that's why a replacement sometimes includes new decking, most often around chimneys, vents and the eaves.

What Each Layer Does
The underlayment is a water-resistant barrier over the whole deck. It's a second barrier for any water that gets past the shingles. Ice and water shield is a self-adhering membrane. It goes along the eaves, in the valleys and around penetrations, where meltwater and wind-driven rain are most likely to back up under the roof. It's often required along the eaves in Indiana.

Drip edge is metal at the roof edge that directs water into the gutter and away from the fascia. Ridge vent lets hot, damp air out at the peak. Poor ventilation is one reason shingles age early and ice dams form, so it's worth checking on every replacement.
Tear-Off or a New Layer?
Putting new shingles over old ones is usually a poor idea. The old roof hides the deck, so soft or rotted wood goes unseen. The extra weight and the extra layer can also shorten the new roof's life, and local code may limit how many layers a house can carry. A full tear-off (stripping everything to the deck) lets us look at every square foot of deck, which is the only way to find what's under the old roof.
Asphalt or Metal
Asphalt shingles are the common choice, and we install GAF Timberline HDZ shingles. Most asphalt replacements in Fort Wayne run from about $7,000 to $22,000, depending on roof size, pitch, complexity and materials.

Metal costs more up front. Metal roofing in Fort Wayne typically runs from $18,000 to $45,000 or more, depending on the panels, the tear-off and the details. The better comparison is service life, upkeep and warranty over the years you'll own the house. Standing seam has concealed fasteners and the longest life. Corrugated costs less and uses exposed fasteners, which need correct placement to hold up. Metal also usually takes longer on site than shingles.

After the Hail
Storm damage isn't always obvious. Hail can bruise shingles and knock off granules, and wind can lift and crease them without tearing them away. The roof may look fine from the yard while its protection is already weaker. Left alone, that kind of damage can lead to leaks, mold and a shorter roof life.

That is why a close look is worth having even when nothing is leaking yet. Hail often leaves collateral signs too, such as dented gutters, vents, ridge caps and other soft metals, and those help show a storm hit your house.
First Steps After a Storm
Please stay off the roof. A wet, steep or damaged roof is dangerous, and your weight can harm shingles that are already weakened. Look from the ground. Walk the yard for pieces of shingle, bent downspouts and debris.

Write down the date of the storm, and the time if you know it. Photograph what you see, with something for scale, before anything gets cleaned up or moved. Then check the ceilings and the attic. If water is coming in, a bucket and a move of your belongings away from the drip can limit the damage. Most policies expect you to take reasonable steps to prevent more damage.
What the Storm Inspection Documents
We don't look from the driveway. We photograph the slopes, gutters, vents, ridge caps, soft metals and any signs of a leak inside. On hail, we use test squares, a standardized method that insurance adjusters recognize. You get photos and video, an estimate for the repair or replacement, and a plain explanation of whether repair, replacement or waiting makes sense.

If we don't find damage, we tell you. We don't invent or exaggerate damage to generate work, and we don't push you to file a claim. If the damage is below your deductible, we explain that.
Who Does What in a Claim
We handle the contractor side: the inspection, the photos, the written estimate, and, when you ask, meeting the adjuster at your house to show what we documented and answer construction questions. Your insurance company decides coverage, scope and payment under your policy. We don't interpret your policy and we don't promise a claim outcome.

After the adjuster visits, your insurer issues its own estimate. We review it against our documentation. When the two differ, we point out the specific differences, such as measurements, drip edge, underlayment, ventilation or flashing, and when it's appropriate we prepare a supplement. A supplement is a request, not a guarantee. The insurer can approve, change or deny each item.
How a Claim Usually Goes
Every policy and every insurer is different, so this is the usual shape, not a promise. You report the damage. The insurer often sends an adjuster, then issues an estimate of what it will cover. You pay a deductible, and some policies use a separate, higher deductible for wind and hail, often a percentage of your dwelling coverage.

Policies commonly pay either replacement cost or actual cash value. With actual cash value, the insurer subtracts depreciation, and the difference can be large on an older roof. Some replacement cost policies hold back depreciation until the work is done. Ask your agent which one you have before you file.

Be Careful With Door-Knockers
After a big storm, out-of-town contractors often go door to door. Not every one of them is a problem, but a few habits are worth watching for. Be cautious about a large deposit before any work starts. Don't sign an assignment of benefits without understanding it, because it can hand control of your claim to the contractor. An offer to waive or absorb your deductible is a red flag, and Indiana law generally treats that as insurance fraud.
