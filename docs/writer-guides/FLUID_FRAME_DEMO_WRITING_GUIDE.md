# Fluid Frame Demo Writing Guide

Write customer-facing pages that make a specific local business’s case: supported benefits, useful decision help, faithful customer quotations, and a clear next step.

The approved Springfield examples in `examples/approved-copy/` are the main teaching. This file is a short affirmative framework. It is not a scoring rubric.

## What the writer should accomplish

- Make the business’s case through **supported customer benefits** and **useful decision help**.
- Use the prospect’s facts, demonstrated behavior, and customer experiences to create **specificity**.
- Place **faithful, attributed customer quotations** beside the benefits they support.
- Match the **scope and confidence** of each claim to the available evidence.
- Fulfill the **approved job of each page** while choosing the flow and expression that suit this business.
- Learn craft, rhythm, depth, and judgment from the examples.
- Write an owner-facing **Strategy Overview** that explains the finished work, separately from the business-facing pages.
- Write a final **SEO title** and **meta description** for each customer-facing routed page (homepage, both service pages, contact). The later builder consumes those words.

The reference businesses’ facts, credentials, offers, and reviews belong to those businesses. They teach craft. The current prospect packet supplies the current business’s evidence.

## SEO title and meta description

These fields are part of the finished `writing-package/v1` page, not a later builder invention.

- Let the approved page job, URL, and primary keyword guide the wording.
- Write a literal, useful result-line the customer can trust. Stay inside confirmed evidence.
- Do not stuff keywords. Do not treat character or pixel counts as a gate. Human QA is the editorial gate.
- Header and footer copy does not get SEO metadata.
- The Strategy Overview is owner-facing review copy, not a search-result page. Omit SEO metadata there unless a human explicitly asks for it.

## Sources of truth

Trust, in this order:

1. Approved page prescription (the job of the page, URL, keyword, and locked decisions)
2. Verified facts, offers, and evidence
3. Verified reviews
4. Finished earlier-stage pages, when writing later in the chain
5. This guide and the page-role guide
6. The approved examples, for craft

Do not reopen strategy decisions the prescription already made. Do not invent facts because a familiar industry claim would read more easily.

Where evidence supports less specificity, make a **narrower useful statement**. That is ordinary writing judgment, not a special exception system and not an experiment to narrate to the customer.

## Upstream ideas are recommendations

Researchers and prescribers offer recommendations with may-language. Writers may use, modify, combine, or set aside those suggestions.

Preserve approved decisions and factual integrity.

Review classifications, suggested first reviews, section sketches, word targets, FAQ counts, and similar editorial aids are **advisory**. They are not permission barriers and not mechanical substitutes for judgment.

Human QA is the editorial gate. Do not hide a removed rulebook in a private prompt.

## Voice boundary

Business-facing pages speak as the business. They help a ready-to-hire local customer decide, trust, and act.

Keep demo, audit, mockup, “we built this page to,” and Fluid Frame commentary off those pages. Those ideas belong only on the Strategy Overview.

The Strategy Overview is owner-facing. It explains the website that actually exists — why this page set, what changed, what the customer can now do. Write it last.

## Decision-stage writing

Assume the reader already knows they need help and is choosing whom to call.

Useful value usually comes from proof, specificity, reassurance, decision help, and a low-friction next step — not from out-contenting competitors with longer generic lists.

Do not spend the page teaching the service category. Do not repeat generic praise to fill space.

## Truth and confidence

Write confirmed facts directly. Do not hedge a supported fact because cautious prose sounds safer.

Do not invent services, areas, contact details, tenure, credentials, awards, warranties, financing, free estimates, emergency coverage, timing, pricing, or outcomes.

A customer experience is not a guarantee. A review can support what that customer experienced; it does not create a company-wide promise.

If the evidence cannot support a promise, state what is actually supported and move on. Do not narrate the limits of the file to the customer.

## Specificity

Prefer the real repair, process, material, behavior, or customer experience over “quality,” “reliable,” “excellence,” or “your trusted partner.”

The reference examples show this without a sentence-pattern list. If a paragraph still could belong to almost any contractor after the name and city are removed, make it more specific or cut it.

## Customer quotations

Quotations are proof placed beside the benefit they support, not a testimonial dump.

Use only verified reviews. Keep attribution. Keep the customer’s wording, or a faithful excerpt. Do not merge reviews, strengthen the claim, or invent a city, service, or result the reviewer did not give.

A suggested first review is a useful starting point, not a placement command. If another verified review clearly serves the written page better, use it.

Spread quotations through the page. Do not clump them. Supporting quotations may prove workmanship, communication, or fairness; they do not all need to repeat the primary keyword.

If suitable reviews exist and a sales page uses none, that is usually a miss — unless the writer has a real editorial reason and human QA agrees.

## Mobile and emphasis

- Short, readable mobile paragraphs. Usually one or two lines where practical; three is the normal upper aim.
- Judge **rendered reading density**, not sentence count.
- Rewrite dense prose when that improves it. Do not merely insert a break.
- Use proper heading hierarchy. Do not add bold to headings or subheadings.
- Use **body bold** when it helps scanning.
- Use italics less often than bold, naturally, without quotas.
- Let lists and paragraph rhythm serve the reader. Not every section should become a list.

## Page flow

The prescription names the job. The writer chooses the sections, order, and expression that serve this customer and this evidence.

Keep the page’s logic consistent enough that a scanner can follow it. Change the order when the decision requires it.

Homepage, service, contact, and header/footer roles are in the page guides. The examples show those roles in three different Springfield businesses.

## Strategy Overview

Write it last, from the finished business-facing site plus the original audit and prescription.

It is a concise owner-facing explanation, not an internal dump and not another sales page for the local customer.
