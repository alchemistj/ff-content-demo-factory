# **Fluid Frame Demo Writing Guide**

## **Purpose**

Write demo-site copy that feels like the prospect already hired Fluid Frame and launched a real production website.  
Business-facing pages should help a ready-to-hire local customer decide, trust, and act. They are not blog posts, audit reports, SEO explanations, or descriptions of the demo itself.  
This guide is intentionally a **spirit-of-the-law guide**. It defines the few things that must stay consistent while leaving intelligent writers room to decide section flow, headings, bullets, transitions, and how proof fits naturally on the page.

## **Source hierarchy**

Use this order when deciding what is true and what the page should do:

> 1. Approved page prescription  
> 2. Verified prospect facts and evidence  
> 3. Verified review inventory and review judgments  
> 4. Finished earlier-stage pages, when writing later in the three-agent chain  
> 5. This overall guide  
> 6. The page-specific guide

Do not reopen strategy decisions the page prescription already made. Do not invent facts because a common industry claim would make the writing easier.  
If a useful fact is not confirmed, omit it or write around it naturally.

## **The three-stage writing chain**

### **Agent 1 — Two service pages**

The service-page writer establishes the deepest business voice first. It receives the full evidence and review packet and writes only the two prescribed service pages.

### **Agent 2 — Homepage, Contact, Header, Footer**

The second writer receives the finished service pages. It should make the homepage summarize and route into those pages rather than restating them. Header, footer, and contact language should sound like the same business.

### **Agent 3 — Strategy Overview**

The Strategy Overview is written last. It receives the entire finished business website plus the original audit and prescription.  
It explains the website that actually exists—not an earlier imagined version of it.

## **Voice boundary**

### **Business-facing pages**

The homepage, service pages, contact page, header, and footer speak as the business.  
Never say things such as:

> * this demo  
> * this mockup  
> * the current website  
> * we built this page to  
> * this page demonstrates  
> * Fluid Frame recommends  
> * this website makes it easier to

Those ideas belong only on the Strategy Overview.

### **Strategy Overview**

The Strategy Overview at / is the only page allowed to discuss:

> * the current site  
> * missed opportunities  
> * why Fluid Frame chose the page set  
> * SEO or conversion gaps  
> * why particular services were elevated  
> * what the completed demo does differently

It should still be concise and prospect-facing. It is a sales explanation, not an internal audit dump.

## **Decision-stage writing**

Assume the reader already knows they need help and is choosing who to contact.  
Write to answer questions such as:

> * Is this the right company for my job?  
> * Do they actually do this kind of work?  
> * Can I trust how they approach it?  
> * What should I expect?  
> * What are my options?  
> * Where do they work?  
> * What should I do next?

Do not spend half the page teaching the reader what the service category is.

## **Above-the-fold shape**

Homepage and primary service pages use this shared rhythm:

> 1. Eyebrow  
> 2. Short, literal, keyword-forward H1  
> 3. Two to three short trust/differentiation sentences  
> 4. Two CTAs with a real commitment gradient

The exact words should fit the business. This is not a sentence template.  
The primary CTA is the higher-commitment action. The secondary CTA gives a useful lower-commitment path.  
Never manufacture an offer to make the CTA stronger.

## **Truth rules**

Never invent:

> * services  
> * service areas  
> * phone numbers or emails  
> * ownership or founder history  
> * tenure  
> * credentials  
> * licenses  
> * awards  
> * brands or manufacturer status  
> * warranties or guarantees  
> * financing  
> * free estimates  
> * emergency or 24/7 availability  
> * same-day service  
> * pricing  
> * response times  
> * customer outcomes  
> * review counts

A claim may be written confidently when the evidence supports it. Do not hedge a confirmed fact just because cautious prose sounds safer.

At the same time, do not turn a customer experience into a guarantee. A review saying someone arrived within an hour can support responsiveness; it does not create a guaranteed one-hour response promise.

**Do not narrate the limits of your evidence to the customer.** If a fact can't support a promise, state what happened and move on. Never write a sentence whose purpose is to disclaim a claim the page didn't make.

## **Specificity over generic praise**

Prefer the real thing the business does over generic adjectives.  
Weak:

> * quality work  
> * reliable service  
> * customer satisfaction  
> * committed to excellence  
> * your trusted partner  
> * competitive pricing

Stronger:

> * the specific repair, installation, inspection, material, process, availability pattern, or customer experience actually supported by evidence

Use the **name-deletion test** when a paragraph feels generic: remove the business name and city. If the paragraph could belong to almost any contractor, make it more specific or delete it.

## **Sentence-level craft**

> * Active voice  
> * Second person as the primary customer POV  
> * Short paragraphs, usually one to three mobile lines  
> * Short, literal headings  
> * AP-style capitalization  
> * Grade 5–6 readability where practical  
> * Bold body phrases sparingly for scanning  
> * No italic body text  
> * No curiosity-gap hooks  
> * No consultant/guru voice  
> * No sensitive-topic banter  
> * One space between sentences

Use bullets when a list improves scanning. Use numbered lists only when sequence matters.

## **Review proof: deterministic requirements**

Reviews are not a decorative testimonial section. They are evidence placed beside the claims they prove.  
The **page evidence grade comes from the upstream prescription/evidence layer. The writer does not assign or downgrade it.**

### **Grade A page**

Use:

> * one lead review  
> * two supporting reviews

Minimum: **3 reviews**.

### **Grade B page**

Use:

> * one lead review  
> * one supporting review

Minimum: **2 reviews**.

### **Grade C page**

Use:

> * one suitable review when suitable evidence exists  
> * zero reviews is allowed only when no suitable review exists

### **Hard zero-review failure**

If suitable review anchors exist for a sales page and the page uses zero reviews, the page fails.  
This is a counting rule, not a stylistic judgment.

### **Recommended first review**

The page prescription should provide a recommended first review when a strong direct match exists.  
Treat that review as the preferred lead evidence, not as an exact-placement command.  
If another verified review clearly works better once the page is written, the writer may use it instead. Record the reason for the change in the copy artifact or stage output.

### **Spacing**

> * Never put two reviews adjacent to one another.  
> * Never create a dedicated testimonial/reviews block that clumps them together.  
> * Spread them through the page as proof and rhythm.

### **Proximity**

Every review must sit within approximately **150 words** of the claim it is assigned to prove.  
The copy artifact/evidence mapping should identify that claim. The text around the review should make the relationship obvious to a human reader.

### **Fidelity**

> * Use only verified reviews.  
> * Preserve reviewer identity.  
> * Preserve exact wording or a faithful excerpt.  
> * Never merge two reviews.  
> * Never strengthen the customer's claim.  
> * Never invent a city, service, result, or context that the reviewer did not give.  
> * Lower-rated reviews remain evidence/traps even when they are not suitable positive proof.

### **Density**

Words-per-review may be tracked on a scorecard so Fluid Frame can see whether proof is getting thin over time.  
**Do not gate copy on a words-per-review ratio.**  
The hard gates are review presence when evidence exists, spacing, proximity, and fidelity.

## **Review roles**

Supporting reviews do not all need to repeat the page's primary service keyword.  
A service page can use:

> * one direct service review  
> * one workmanship review  
> * one pricing or communication review

What matters is that each review earns its place by proving a nearby claim.

## **Images and writing**

The writer may use approved visual evidence to understand the business and may recommend a useful image subject or prospect-owned image URL when it genuinely supports a section.  
Do not micromanage layout or prescribe exact image coordinates.  
For later build stages:

> * prospect-owned public website images may be used below the fold  
> * they are never used as heroes  
> * they should receive visible source attribution  
> * a remote-image failure must collapse cleanly without a broken-image box

The writer should never depend on an image as the only support for a factual claim if the image itself does not clearly establish that fact.

## **SEO alignment**

Respect the approved:

> * URL  
> * primary keyword  
> * page intent  
> * proposed title direction  
> * H1 direction

Use the primary keyword naturally. Do not make the homepage compete with a dedicated service page. Do not make sibling service pages restate each other's intent.  
SEO title and meta description should be literal, useful, and based only on confirmed claims.

## **Cross-page continuity**

Later writers should treat finished earlier pages as context, not raw material to copy.  
The homepage should summarize and route into service pages.  
Service pages should go deeper than the homepage and stay distinct from one another.  
The Strategy Overview should accurately describe the final page set and the decisions visible in the finished website.

## **Writer self-QA**

Before returning a page, check:

> * Does it sound like the business itself?  
> * Is every important claim supported?  
> * Is the page specific to this business and this customer decision?  
> * Did I accidentally invent an offer or promise?  
> * Is the intended keyword/URL job still clear?  
> * Did suitable review proof actually make it onto the page?  
> * Do review count, spacing, proximity, and fidelity pass?  
> * Are sibling pages meaningfully different?  
> * Did I use agency rather than blindly filling a template?
