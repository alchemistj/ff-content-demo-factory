# How to write a page

Josh, 2026-11-09. Read this first. It is the method, not a rulebook. No guide can list every "don't", so this one explains what good pages do and why, and shows one real before-and-after. Use judgment.

The finished model for a multi-service homepage is [examples/approved-copy/evolve-exteriors/homepage.md](../../examples/approved-copy/evolve-exteriors/homepage.md). The Springfield pages in `examples/approved-copy/` show the same craft on other trades.

## What good pages do

1. **They argue one idea.** The page has a point of view the whole business can stand behind, and every section serves it. Window Dudes: "Inspect First. Fix Right." Evolve: the outside of a house is one system, so one company does all of it and an owner looks first. Find the idea in the reviews and the facts, not in a slogan list.
2. **They start from the homeowner's moment.** A room that never gets comfortable. Curtains that move with the window shut. Shoving a patio door open with your shoulder. Name the thing the visitor is living with before you name the service.
3. **They explain why.** Fog between the panes means the seal failed. Siding takes the wind and hail first. Sliders leak because rollers wear and the track bends. General trade knowledge like this is allowed and expected; it is not a claim about the business. Claims about the business (warranty, years, services, people, prices) still come only from `facts.md`.
4. **They give real answers.** "Most window jobs are finished in a day" beats "We give you the schedule at your estimate." Answer the question the visitor actually has, from the facts and the pattern in the reviews. Defer only what truly depends on their house.
5. **They let reviews prove a point.** Pick the review that proves the paragraph right above it, and let it run long enough to tell its story. Two to five reviews on a page, each placed where it earns its spot. A review is never a spacer between sections.
6. **They are specific to this business.** If a sentence could sit on any competitor's site after you swap the name and city, make it specific or cut it.
7. **They use short paragraphs.** One to three sentences, usually one or two lines on a phone. Break a long block wherever the thought turns. Lists are fine when the content is a list; reasoning stays in sentences.
8. **They have depth.** A homepage that teaches runs about 900 to 1,300 words of copy, not counting reviews, menus or footer. A service page about 700 to 1,100. Length comes from teaching and answering, never from padding.

Voice is the business speaking as "we" to one visitor as "you". Owners speak in the first person ("We're Colton and Riley").

## The steps

### 1. Plan the page (before any copy)
Write a short plan in `plan.md`:
- **The idea:** one or two sentences the whole page argues.
- **For each section:** the homeowner's moment, the reason behind it, what the business does about it, and the review that proves it (reviewer and the line you'll use).
- **The questions** a visitor would ask before calling, with the real answer for each from the facts.

If you can't fill a line from the facts and reviews, the section probably doesn't belong.

### 2. Write the page as a page
Write in plain Markdown, the way the examples read: headings, short paragraphs, reviews as block quotes, links where a service has its own page. Don't think in template boxes; the conversion into the site format comes later.

**The top of every page ends in a call to action** (Josh, 2026-11-09). The first screen is the eyebrow, the H1, one short paragraph (two or three sentences) that ends by telling the visitor what to do ("Call me for a free estimate."), then the two buttons on their own line:

`**Buttons:** Call (316) 555-1234 | Get a Free Estimate`

One paragraph only, so the buttons stay on a phone's first screen.

### 3. Read it as the owner and as the visitor
Read it aloud in your head as the owner. Would they say this? Is anything invented, hedged or generic? Then read it as someone with a drafty house on their phone. Do they learn something? Do they know what to do next?

## One real before-and-after (Evolve Exteriors homepage)

**Before:** 250 words of copy, four one-line reviews as spacers.
> **You Deal With the Owners.** We stay close to every job ourselves. One of us often quotes your home in person, and we check back once the work is done.
> > "My house is much quieter and cooler now." — Russell M.

**After:** each service gets the visitor's moment, the reason, what we do, and a review that proves it.
> ## Sliding Patio Doors
> An old sliding door is usually the leakiest opening in the house. The rollers wear out, the track bends, and the seal where the panels meet stops sealing.
>
> You end up shoving it open with your shoulder and feeling the draft all winter. A new slider glides on new rollers and seals tight when it's closed.
>
> > "I now can open my sliding glass door with 1 finger instead of putting my whole body in it." — Karla S.

Why it's better: the reader recognizes their own door, learns why it fails, and the review proves the fix in one vivid line.

**A detour worth knowing:** the first rewrite was all windows. Good window copy, wrong page. The homepage keyword was "exterior remodeling company", so the homepage sells the whole exterior and each service gets its own section. Window depth belongs on the window page. Match the page to its keyword.

**And the paragraphs:** the second draft had five- and six-sentence blocks. Josh: "I like short paragraphs." The approved version breaks every block at the turn of the thought.

## Things that pull copy flat
- Filling boxes instead of writing a page.
- Stating what the business does instead of what the visitor is dealing with.
- Claims with no reason ("New siding is a sure way to lower your energy bills").
- A stat placed right above the review that already says it. Say the benefit; let the review carry the specifics.
- "Customers tell us…" and "Customers describe…" sentences. State the benefit in our voice; the review proves it.
- Answering a question with "ask us at your estimate."
