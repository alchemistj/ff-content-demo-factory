How Long Does a Shingle Roof Last in Indiana? (Is 20 Years Too Old?)
How Long Does a Shingle Roof Last in Indiana? (Is 20 Years Too Old?)
By Affordable Roofing · Published Nov 2, 2026
The short answer: A well-installed architectural shingle roof in Indiana lasts about 25 years, and older 3-tab shingle roofs usually wear out sooner. InterNACHI, the home inspectors' association, lists about 20 years for 3-tab shingles under normal conditions. Real life is shorter or longer depending on the installation, the attic ventilation, and how much sun, wind, hail and ice the roof takes. So a 20-year-old roof isn't automatically finished, but it's old enough that you should know its condition and start planning.
The short version:
3-tab shingles: about 20 years under normal conditions (InterNACHI). Many 20-year-old 3-tab roofs around Fort Wayne are at the end.
Architectural shingles like Owens Corning Duration and Oakridge: about 25 years in northeast Indiana, based on what we see on Fort Wayne roofs.
The warranty isn't the lifespan. A "30-year" or "lifetime" label describes the manufacturer's limited warranty, not a promise of how long the roof will look and work its best.
What shortens a roof's life: poor attic ventilation, a weak or wet deck, installation shortcuts, two layers of shingles, and storm damage.
At 15 to 20 years, get the roof looked at and start budgeting. At 20+ on 3-tab, plan on replacement.

How long do asphalt shingles last, by type?
Not all shingle roofs are the same. The type on your house is the first clue to how long it will last.
Roof type	Typical life under normal conditions	What it looks like from the street
3-tab asphalt shingles	About 20 years	Flat, single layer with evenly spaced cutouts (tabs) in a regular pattern
Architectural (dimensional) asphalt shingles	About 25 years in northeast Indiana (our experience)	Thicker, layered look with shadow lines and no uniform tabs
Metal roofing	About 40 to 80 years	Panels or standing seams
Source for 3-tab: InterNACHI's Standard Estimated Life Expectancy Chart for Homes. The architectural figure is our own experience on Fort Wayne-area roofs. InterNACHI says these numbers assume regular maintenance and normal wear and tear, not extreme weather, and are guidelines, not guarantees.
InterNACHI also notes that "the life of a roof depends on local weather conditions, building and design, material quality, and adequate maintenance." That's why two houses built the same year on the same street can need new roofs years apart.

Is a 20-year-old roof too old?
It depends on what's on the house:
20-year-old 3-tab roof: it has reached the typical lifespan. If you're also seeing curling, bald spots or granules in the gutters, it's time for a new roof.
20-year-old architectural roof: it may have years left if it was installed well and the attic breathes. It's still worth a close look, especially after a hard winter or a hailstorm.
You don't know the age or type: find out. Check your closing papers, a home inspection report, or a past permit record.
Age matters for more than wear. When you're selling a house, buyers and their inspectors look at the roof's age. If you plan to stay, a roof near the end of its life is easier to replace on your schedule than after a storm forces the issue.

What about a 10-year-old roof?
A 10-year-old roof should normally have plenty of life left. If a roof that young is already curling, losing lots of granules or blowing shingles off, something else is usually going on. The common causes are poor attic ventilation, installation problems, or storm damage. It's worth having someone look at it rather than assuming shingles just wear out that fast.
Does a "30-year" or "lifetime" shingle really last that long?
Not necessarily. The number on the shingle wrapper describes the manufacturer's limited warranty. It doesn't promise the roof will last that long on your house.
For example, Owens Corning Duration and Oakridge both carry the Owens Corning Limited Lifetime shingle warranty for as long as you own the home. The warranty covers specific manufacturing defects under specific conditions. It doesn't stop wind, hail, ice dams or a poorly vented attic from wearing a roof out early. Always read the actual warranty for the coverage details, limitations and requirements.
What a strong shingle line does give you is a better starting point. Duration's SureNail strip gives it a 130-mph wind warranty with standard four-nail installation, according to Owens Corning, and that holding power matters in northeast Indiana storms.

What shortens the life of a shingle roof in Indiana?
Ranch home roof replacement with Owens Corning Duration shingles.
Freeze and thaw. Northeast Indiana winters bring snow, ice and repeated freezing and thawing. Snow that melts and refreezes at the eaves can form ice dams. Owens Corning explains that ice damming "can be a sign of poor attic ventilation" and can push water back into the attic. Ice and water protection at the eaves is part of the roof system for this reason.
Summer heat and sun. InterNACHI notes that heat shortens asphalt shingle life. A hot attic bakes shingles from below. South- and west-facing slopes usually wear first because they take the most afternoon sun.
Attic ventilation. A roof is a system. Air needs to come in low (at the soffits) and leave high (at the ridge or roof vents). When it can't, heat and moisture build up, which ages shingles and can damage the deck. When we replace a roof, ventilation is part of the job.
Wind and hail. Spring and summer storms can lift, crease or tear off shingles and bruise them with hail. A roof that gets hit hard can lose years even if it doesn't leak right away.
Two layers of shingles. Some older homes have a second layer of shingles nailed over the first. It traps heat, adds weight and hides problems in the deck. On our full replacements, we tear off every layer down to the deck.
Installation shortcuts. Nails outside the manufacturer's nailing zone, skipped starter shingles, or missing underlayment can shorten a roof's life no matter how good the shingle is. Owens Corning's warranty also depends on the shingles being installed to its instructions.

How to get the most years out of your next roof
You can't change the weather, but you can control most of what decides how long a new roof lasts:
Pick an architectural shingle. Our asphalt shingle roofing page explains the Owens Corning lines we install.
Tear off down to the deck. Ask any roofer whether the quote includes a full tear-off and how bad decking is handled.
Get the ventilation right. The quote should mention intake and exhaust ventilation, not just shingles.
Make sure ice and water protection is included at the eaves and in the valleys.
Hire an installer the manufacturer trusts. Owens Corning Preferred Contractors are the ones who can offer the upgraded 10-year workmanship warranty.
Keep the gutters clear so water and ice don't back up at the eaves.

Frequently asked questions
How long does a shingle roof last in Indiana?
Under normal conditions, InterNACHI lists about 20 years for 3-tab asphalt shingles, and in our experience architectural shingles in northeast Indiana last about 25 years. Indiana roofs take freeze-thaw winters, summer heat, wind and hail, so the real number depends on the installation, attic ventilation and storm history. A professional look tells you more than the age alone.
Is a 20-year-old roof too old?
For a 3-tab roof, 20 years is about the typical lifespan, so plan on replacement, especially if you see curling or bald spots. A 20-year-old architectural roof may have years left if it was installed well and vented properly. Either way, it's old enough to get a free estimate and start budgeting.
How long does a 30-year shingle really last?
"30-year" describes the manufacturer's limited warranty, not a guaranteed lifespan. In our experience, architectural shingles in northeast Indiana last about 25 years, and poor ventilation, a bad deck, storms or installation shortcuts can shorten it. Read the actual warranty for what it covers.
Should a 10-year-old roof need replacing?
Usually not. A 10-year-old roof should have plenty of life left. If it's already curling, shedding lots of granules or losing shingles, the cause is often poor attic ventilation, installation problems or storm damage. Have it looked at before assuming it's just worn out.
