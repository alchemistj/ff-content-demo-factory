Metal Roof Colors and Finishes: What Holds Up and What Fades
PVDF versus SMP paint, light versus dark colors in a Midwest climate, gloss and oil canning, HOAs, and why a screen never shows you the real color.
Color is the decision homeowners enjoy most and the one they spend the least time on where it counts. The color you see is paint, and the paint is a chemistry choice that decides whether the roof looks the same in 25 years or has chalked to a lighter, duller version of itself. Let me start there, then get to the part that is actually a matter of taste.
The finish under the color
Residential metal roofing comes with two common paint systems, and the difference is not visible on day one.
PVDF, sold under names like Kynar 500, is a fluoropolymer resin. It holds color and gloss for decades and resists chalking. On our jobs it is the finish on interlocking metal shingles, and it can be had on 24-gauge standing seam steel if you ask for it. It is tested against a list of ASTM standards for color change, chalk, gloss, humidity, salt spray, flexibility, and adhesion.
SMP, silicone-modified polyester, is the cheaper paint and the one most often found on exposed-fastener panels sold by the sheet. It is harder and scratches a little less easily, and it fades and chalks noticeably sooner. On a barn nobody minds. On a house you will look at every day for 40 years, it is the wrong place to save money.
There is also bare Galvalume, unpainted steel with its own aluminum-zinc coating. It weathers to a matte gray and has a place on agricultural buildings. Most homeowners do not want it on a house.
Ask for the paint system by name. If the answer is "it's painted," ask again. More on coatings.
Light or dark, in this climate
Darker colors absorb more heat. Lighter colors reflect more of it. That is true, and in Texas it matters a lot. In Indiana, Ohio, and Michigan it matters less than people assume, because our heating season is longer than our cooling season and a dark roof gives back a little in winter, warming faster in low sun and shedding snow sooner. Modern PVDF finishes also carry reflective pigments across most of the color range, so a dark bronze today reflects more than a dark bronze did 20 years ago.
My honest guidance: pick the color for the house, not for the utility bill. The energy difference between two colors on a well-ventilated attic is small next to the difference ventilation and insulation make. If efficiency is high on your list, the energy article covers what actually moves the number.
Gloss, texture, and oil canning
Low-gloss and matte finishes hide more. A high-gloss dark panel shows every ripple, and flat metal always has some ripple. That waviness is called oil canning. It is cosmetic, it does not affect the roof, and it is more visible on wide flat pans in dark glossy colors under a low sun. Striations or a small rib in the panel break it up. If you want a dark standing seam roof, ask to see that combination on a real house before you commit.
Textured and wrinkle finishes exist and do the same job on metal shingles, along with helping them read as shake or slate from the street.
Screens lie about color
This is the part I say to everyone. Color and texture always look different on a screen than on a roof. A chip in your hand in direct sun is closer. A full panel leaned against your siding at the time of day you usually see your house is closest. The gallery is useful for the shape of a roof and the general family of a color. It is not useful for the exact shade. Get physical samples.
Neighborhoods and HOAs
Metal shingles in a slate or shake color blend into an established street. A standing seam roof in matte black announces itself. Neither is wrong, but one of them may need your HOA's approval, and we can provide product data and samples for that. If you are not sure what the neighbors will think, drive the neighborhood and look at which roofs you notice and which you do not.
Who this decision is not for
If your plan is to repaint the roof in ten years for a new look, metal is the wrong material. A factory PVDF finish is not something you repaint on a schedule. Field-applied paint is a maintenance cycle, three to five years between coats, and you would be trading a 40-year finish for that. Pick a color you can live with for decades, or pick a different roof.
The rest is taste, and taste is allowed to decide. Now you have the context to choose well.
