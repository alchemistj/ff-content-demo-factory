Portage roofs are mostly simpler — the snow is not
Portage is largely postwar and later suburban housing — ranches, split-levels, and two-stories on hips and gables, sound framing, usually on their second or third asphalt roof. Those are straightforward metal installs. The weather is not: Portage sits in the lake-effect belt and gets heavy, wet snow all winter.
Wet snow sits on low-slope sections and melts and refreezes at the eave over and over. On asphalt that water only needs a nail head. On a mechanically seamed panel there is no fastener head or horizontal seam for it to find. Metal does not stop ice forming — the membrane at the eave and the attic ventilation do the real work — but it changes what the ice can get into.
Which system, on which house
On a simple gable or hip, standing seam formed on site to the full length of the run is the cleanest answer — no horizontal seams, concealed clips, nothing in the weather. If you want the roof to still read as shingles from the street, an interlocking metal shingle does that and wants a 3/12 pitch or better. The lake homes around Portage are where I think hardest about wind and sliding snow: a concealed-clip seam holds up on an open-water lot, and snow retention above a deck or a door belongs in the quote from the start.
Exposed fastener is right for a shed and wrong for a house here — the washers under the screws wear out around year ten to twelve, and with this much wet snow I do not want water relying on a washer. We do not install it below 4/12 anywhere.
Spring storms bring hail. Metal stays watertight after hail while sometimes showing cosmetic dents on a broad flat pan. A dent is not a leak, but if a flawless surface for thirty years is what you are picturing, adjust that picture now.
Who this is not for
Selling in a few years — asphalt. One failing detail on a sound roof — a repair contractor; we do not take repair calls. Wants shingles again — a different contractor.
