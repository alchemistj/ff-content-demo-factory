Ribbed Metal Roofing: What You Get and What Wears Out
The exposed-fastener panel most people picture as a metal roof: what it costs less for, why the washers are the whole story, the pitch limit, who it fits.
Ribbed panel is the roof most people picture when they hear "metal roof," the straight lines and exposed screws you see on barns and pole buildings. It is the least expensive metal roof we install, and it goes on fast. Here's the tradeoff: every one of those screws has a rubber washer under it, and those washers wear out. That is the whole story of this system, so let me start there.
What it is
Wide steel panels, usually 36 inches of coverage, with raised ribs every 9 or 12 inches. Panels overlap at a rib and are screwed through the face into the deck or purlins, a screw every foot or so along every rib line. The screw head and its rubber washer are the seal. Ours is the 4Ever Guard, and there is more on the exposed fastener page.
It is sometimes called ag panel, R-panel, PBR, or corrugated, though corrugated is strictly the wavy profile. What they share is the exposed fastener, and that is what matters.
What it does well
It costs less than any other metal roof, because the panel is simpler to make, there is no seaming equipment, and a crew can cover a lot of roof in a day. It has a long service history. The steel and the paint, if it is a real PVDF finish on decent gauge, last as long as on any other system. For a barn, a shop, a pole building, or a house where the budget is the binding constraint, it is a real roof.
Where it fails
The screws. Several hundred of them, each with a rubber washer sitting in direct sun, and each panel expanding and contracting under the screw head every day of the year. The washers harden and crack. The holes work loose. Somewhere around year ten to twelve the fasteners need inspection and replacement, often with a larger screw because the original hole has wallowed out. Skip that and the roof leaks at the screws long before the steel is done.
Two things make it worse. Low pitch, because slow water spends more time on every screw, which is why most manufacturers limit the warranty below 3/12 and we will not install it below 4/12. And bad driving, overdriven so the washer is crushed or underdriven so it never seals. Both leak, on a schedule.
I have yet to find an exposed fastener design that is impervious to the elements. Claims about special or protected fasteners do not change that. In real-world use they all perform about the same over time.
What maintenance looks like
A look at the screws every year or two, from a ladder or with binoculars. Backed-out heads, washers that have split or squeezed out, rust rings around a fastener. A fastener service around year ten to twelve. Valleys and penetrations kept clear like any roof. This is a roof that asks to be looked at, and if that is not going to happen, it is the wrong system.
Compared to standing seam
A standing seam roof hides every fastener under a folded seam on a concealed clip, so nothing sealing the roof is in the weather and the panel can move freely. It costs more and takes a crew with the equipment and the training. That difference in price is largely the difference between a roof you service and a roof you do not. How the seam is formed lays the systems side by side.
One practical note: solar, satellite mounts, and snow guards clamp to a standing seam without a hole. On a ribbed panel they screw through the face, which is another fastener to maintain.
Who it fits, and who it does not
It fits outbuildings, agricultural buildings, and homes where budget and speed are the top priorities, on a roof at 4/12 or steeper, with a homeowner who accepts the maintenance and has realistic expectations. It is a good roof for that.
It does not fit a low-pitched roof. It does not fit someone who wants a roof they never think about again. And it does not fit the homeowner who was told "metal roof" and pictured the lifespan of standing seam at the price of a barn roof. If that is what you are expecting, this system may not be the right fit, and I would rather say so now.
Now you have the context to decide.
