What a Roof Warranty Actually Covers
Why a 30-year shingle warranty rarely pays, the exclusions in the fine print, what a materials-and-workmanship warranty means, and what no warranty can do.
Most homeowners have been promised a "30-year" or "lifetime" shingle warranty at some point, and almost none of them have collected on one. That is not bad luck. It is how those documents are written. Here is what a shingle warranty actually covers, what ours covers and why, and the thing no warranty on earth can do for you.
The shingle warranty, read closely
Start with the number. "30-year" on a shingle wrapper is a warranty term, not a lifespan. The shingle is rated to last 12 to 20 years here, and the warranty period runs past that on purpose, because of what it does not cover.
Proration. Most shingle warranties reduce the value of the coverage every year, and some every month. Full coverage lasts a few years. After that, a claim at year 15 on a "30-year" shingle pays a fraction of the material cost, and only the material.
What is excluded. Labor to remove the failed roof. Tear-off and disposal. Underlayment. And then the list of causes: improper ventilation, ice dams, foot traffic, incorrect fastening, and anything judged to be the installer's fault rather than the shingle's. Read that list again. Those are most of the things that actually cause a roof to fail. The shingle warranty covers a manufacturing defect in the shingle itself, which is rare, and excludes the ways roofs actually leak.
The result is an industry figure that less than 1 percent of asphalt warranty claims are ever paid. The paper is long. The coverage is thin.
Material versus workmanship
This is the distinction that matters on any roof, metal included. A material warranty comes from the manufacturer and covers the product: the steel, the finish, the shingle. A workmanship warranty covers the install: the flashing, the fasteners, the seams, the ventilation. Almost every roof failure I am called to look at is workmanship. The panel is fine. The detail around the chimney was caulked instead of built.
Most roofing companies can offer you a material warranty, because the manufacturer writes it. Fewer can offer a workmanship warranty that a manufacturer stands behind, and here is why. A manufacturer will only back the install when it was done to their spec by a crew they trained and certified. A crew that installs asphalt on Monday and metal on Thursday is rarely certified to install either to that standard. Certification is not a sticker. It is what unlocks the workmanship coverage.
What ours is, and why it can be
Every 4Ever metal roof comes with a lifetime transferable warranty covering materials and workmanship, non-prorated. It covers the metal system and its factory coil coating, and it covers how we put it on. If it fails because of the product or the install, we fix it. That is the whole statement, and the reason we can make it is that our crews are manufacturer-certified, so the manufacturer stands behind the workmanship and not just the panel.
Transferable means the next owner inherits the same coverage. On a roof meant to outlast your time in the house, that matters, and it is one of the few warranty features that shows up at resale. The full terms are on the warranty page.
What no warranty can do
Being honest: no warranty makes a roof maintenance-free, and no roof is zero maintenance. Exposed-fastener systems in particular need their washers looked at on a schedule. A warranty is protection against defects and installation failure. It is not a substitute for keeping the valleys clear, and it is not insurance against a tree. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling, not explaining.
A warranty also does not outlive the company behind it. A lifetime workmanship warranty from a contractor who is gone in five years is a piece of paper. When you compare warranties, you are comparing companies as much as documents.
Questions to ask about any roof warranty
Is it prorated, and if so, from what year?
Does it cover labor, tear-off, and disposal, or material only?
What is the list of exclusions, and is ventilation, ice damming, or fastening on it?
Who backs the workmanship, the contractor alone or the manufacturer through certification?
Is it transferable, and what does the transfer require?
A contractor who answers those plainly is one you can work with. One who hands you a brochure is one to read carefully.
Who this matters for
If you are buying a short-term roof for a short-term hold, the warranty barely matters; you will not be there to claim on it. If you are buying a roof to be the last one, the warranty is a large part of what you are paying for, and the difference between material-only and materials-and-workmanship is the difference between a promise about the steel and a promise about the roof.
Now you have the context to read the fine print.
