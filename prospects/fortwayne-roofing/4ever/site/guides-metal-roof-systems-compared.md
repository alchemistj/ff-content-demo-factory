The metal roof systems sold for houses, compared honestly
Exposed fastener, snap-lock, mechanically seamed standing seam, interlocking shingles and stone-coated steel — what each is, where it fails, who it fits.
Most of the confusion about metal roofing comes from one word doing too much work. "Metal roof" covers a cheap barn panel and a mechanically seamed system that will outlast the house, and salespeople are happy to let you blur the two. This is the explanation I give across the table, in the order I give it.
There are several metal roof systems commonly recommended for homes. Not every system performs well on every roof design or in every climate. And confident recommendations are not always based on long-term performance — many homeowners learn later that a recommendation was based on what the installer was comfortable with, not what was best for the house.
Exposed fastener panels (what we call 4Ever Guard)
What it is. Ribbed steel panels screwed through the face into the deck, with a rubber-washered screw sealing each hole. It was adapted from wall and siding use because it is fast and cheap, and it has a long service history.
What it does well. Lowest upfront cost of any metal roof. Proven construction. Simpler installation — no seaming equipment — which is part of why it costs less. A practical option when affordability is the main priority.
Where it fails. The screws. Every fastener sits in sun, snow and ice, and every panel expands and contracts around it, working the hole and aging the washer. The fasteners and washers wear out long before the panel does. It needs an inspection every year and the fasteners typically need replacing in 10 to 12 years. Most manufacturers limit warranties below a 3/12 pitch. We do not install it below 4/12.
My opinion. It makes sense when budget and speed are the top priorities and you accept the maintenance. Claims about special or protected fasteners do not eliminate the exposure. In real use, every exposed-fastener design I have seen performs about the same over time.
Snap-lock standing seam
What it is. Vertical panels with raised seams that snap onto each other during installation. The seams are not mechanically folded or crimped closed. Clean, modern look; faster to install than a mechanically seamed roof.
Where it fails. Because the seam is not sealed shut, it has limited resistance to standing water, snow and ice. Wind-driven rain can enter the female leg of the seam. Performance depends heavily on pitch and drainage — it is best suited to roofs where water moves off continuously.
My opinion. It can work well on a steep, well-draining roof. It is not a universal standing seam solution, and it carries a higher risk where snow, ice or standing water exist. In those conditions I want a mechanically seamed panel. How the seam is formed matters more than the word "standing seam."
Mechanically seamed standing seam (4Ever Armored)
What it is. A 1.5-inch standing seam panel fastened with concealed clips — not screws through the face — whose seams are folded and locked closed with a seaming machine after the panels are down. The clips allow the panel to expand and contract while the seam stays locked. Panels are roll-formed on site to the exact length of each roof plane.
What it does well. Manages water, snow and movement better than snap-lock or exposed-fastener systems. Works on very low slopes and near-flat applications when designed for it. Handles complex roofs. Needs far less ongoing maintenance.
The tradeoff. More labor, more skill, specialized equipment. It costs more than exposed fastener and about the same as a good interlocking shingle. Fewer crews do it well.
My opinion. This is my most trusted standing seam system for a house. It is the best option when little or no maintenance is the goal. Good design and proper installation still matter more than anything a sales sheet says.
Interlocking metal shingles (4Ever Fortress)
What it is. Painted modular steel shingles with a slate or shake appearance that interlock on all four sides and are installed as a true system, not as overlapping pieces. The lock limits water entry instead of managing water after it gets in.
The tradeoffs. Higher upfront cost than exposed fastener — about the same as mechanically seamed standing seam. It needs a skilled, experienced crew, and there are not many. Few manufacturers make one with well-designed water management. Minimum pitch is 3/12.
My opinion. The best option for a homeowner who wants shingle aesthetics with metal performance. The four-side interlock means less reliance on the underlayment, and that is a performance-first design.
Stone-coated steel
What it is. Steel panels coated with stone granules, stamped to resemble asphalt, shake or tile. Installed as overlapping panels that use open channels underneath to manage water that gets past the surface. Water is managed after it enters the system.
My opinion. Often chosen for the familiar look. Because it is designed to manage water after entry, it relies heavily on the underlayment and the detailing, which is more opportunities for water intrusion over the long term. Pitch has to be above 3/12. I am cautious recommending these systems for long-term residential use, and I install them for homeowners who have weighed that and want the look.
The one idea underneath all of this
Keeping water out is more reliable than managing it later. Every system above sits somewhere on that line: exposed fastener and stone-coated manage water after it gets in; snap-lock mostly keeps it out if the roof is steep; a mechanically locked seam or a four-side interlock keeps it out at the surface.
That is also roughly the order of cost, which is the tradeoff in one sentence: you pay more up front for a system that asks less of the underlayment, the flashing and you.
Now you have the context to read any quote — mine included — and ask the questions that matter: Which seam? What pitch? What keeps the water out, and what happens when it gets in?
