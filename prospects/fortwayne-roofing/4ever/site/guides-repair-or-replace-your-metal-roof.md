Repair or replace? How to read the condition of your roof
How to tell whether a targeted repair will hold or replacement is the better use of your money — fasteners, flashing, coating, decking, pattern.
Not every problem roof needs to come off. And not every roof worth patching is actually worth patching. Here is how I think through it when I am on a roof, so you can think through it too — and so you can tell when someone is converting a repair into a replacement, or the other way around.
Read this knowing that 4Ever installs metal roofs and does not take standalone repair calls. That means the honest outcome of this guide, for a lot of people, is "call a repair contractor." I am fine with that. A sound roof with one bad detail is not an upgrade case no matter how it gets framed.
First, what kind of roof are you reading
The question is different for the three roofs people usually ask me about.
An exposed-fastener metal roof — the screw-down panel you see on barns and a lot of older houses — ages on a clock. Every screw went in the same week with the same neoprene washer, and the washers harden and shrink around year ten to twelve. The roof is not the panel; it is six hundred small seals, all the same age.
A concealed-fastener metal roof — standing seam or interlocking metal shingle — has nothing sealing it that sits in the weather. When one of these leaks, it is nearly always a detail: a flashing, a boot, a valley, an eave. The field is rarely the problem.
An asphalt roof near the end of its life fails all at once rather than gradually: granules in the gutters, curling at the edges, mat showing through. It will take a repair, and the repair holds until the next wind finds the next weak spot.
Which of those you have changes what "repair" even means.
Start at the fasteners
On an exposed-fastener roof, the screws and their washers are usually the first thing to go. If a handful are backing out or weeping on the slope that takes the afternoon sun, that is often a repair — the failed screws get replaced a size up, with new washers, so the threads bite fresh metal.
If it is happening across the whole roof, you are looking at a system reaching the end of its service life, and replacing screws one storm at a time gets expensive fast. Re-screwing the entire field is a real option while the coating is still sound. It stops making sense when the finish has chalked out or the holes have wallowed.
Look at the seams and flashing
Leaks start at transitions: seams, valleys, chimneys, and where the roof meets a wall. A localized flashing failure is frequently repairable — one sidewall done in caulk, one cracked pipe boot, one short valley. Those have names and known fixes.
Widespread seam failure on a poorly installed roof usually is not, because the problem is the installation, not one spot. If every sidewall on the house was flashed with sealant, you do not have one leak; you have a roof full of joints reaching the end of their life together.
Check the coating and the steel
Run your hand across a panel. Heavy chalk on your palm means the finish is wearing through. Look at the cut edges, the laps and the screw holes for rust creeping in. A coating that is done protecting the steel changes the math on every repair above it — you would be putting good screws and good flashing onto a panel that is on its way out.
Check what is underneath
If water has been getting in for a while, the decking below may be compromised. A roof that looks like a surface repair can turn into a bigger job once you see the deck. This is the part you cannot see from the ground, and it is why a real assessment includes the attic — wet sheathing, rusty nail tips, a stained rafter. Deck rot spreading past the immediate leak means the scope has already changed underneath you.
Do the math on repeated repairs
Here is where budgets fall apart. If you are repairing the same roof every year or two, you are financing a replacement in installments and getting none of the benefit. At some point the honest answer is that repair is throwing good money after bad.
The right unit is cost per year of protection, not the repair price against the replacement price. A repair that buys three years is a good buy on a roof with twenty left in it and a poor one on a roof with three.
Here is the tradeoff
A repair fixes the detail that failed. It does nothing for the identical details next to it that are the same age, and a repaired spot rarely matches the weathered finish around it. A formed-metal fix lasts; a sealant patch buys a few years at best.
A replacement resets the whole roof and costs a great deal more. Making that decision under an active leak is a poor condition to make it in — get the water stopped first, then have the larger conversation on your own timeline if it turns out to still be worth having.
When repair is the right call
All three of these are true: the problem is local and has a name; the panels and coating around it are sound; the decking underneath is dry. A relatively young roof with a single failure point almost always qualifies. A ten-year-old metal roof with one bad flashing is a repair, and I am not going to tell you otherwise.
That work goes to a repair contractor. There are capable ones in every market I work in, and the leak guide describes what good repair work looks like in enough detail that you can judge an estimate before you accept it.
When replacement is the better use of the money
Two or more of these: the same failure repeating on every slope; a coating that has chalked out with rust at the edges; deck rot beyond the leak; a screw-down roof at twenty-plus years where the washers that have not let go yet are not far behind; an asphalt roof past the point where a repair buys more than months.
That is the conversation we do have. An assessment may still come back saying the roof is fine and the fix is small. That answer costs you nothing, and it is the one I would rather give.
What a proper assessment looks at
Whoever looks at your roof — us or anyone else — the work should cover more than the drip. Trace the water from the attic side first. Check every roof-to-wall junction and the chimney. Sample the fastener field on more than one slope. Look at the valleys, the boots and the other penetrations. Note the condition of the coating, any rust at cut edges and laps, whether the decking is sound, and how the eave was detailed against ice dams.
A seasonal look — spring and fall, and after any storm that took shingles off a neighbor — catches most of this early, from the ground with binoculars and from inside the attic. You do not need to be on the roof for it.
If whoever you call goes straight to a quote without getting into the attic or onto the specific details, you have learned something about the quote.
Who should not be calling us
Your roof has plenty of life left and one bad detail. Your budget is a repair budget. It is an asphalt roof and you intend to keep it asphalt — we install metal only, and we will say that rather than quote you something you did not ask about. You are moving in the next couple of years, and the case for metal is built on decades you will not be there for.
What this guide cannot tell you
It cannot tell you which case your roof is. Nobody can make this call from the driveway, including me. It takes eyes on the specific details, and ideally in the attic.
What it can do is give you the checklist, so that whoever you call has to answer to it. This is usually where the decision becomes clear.
