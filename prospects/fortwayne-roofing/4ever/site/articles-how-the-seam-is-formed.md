How the Seam Is Formed: Why It Decides the Roof
Exposed fastener, snap-lock, mechanically seamed, and interlocking shingle: how each joint handles water, and why the seam is what to compare in a quote.
If you remember one thing about metal roofing, make it this: how the seam is formed matters. It is the biggest difference between systems, and it is the part that decides how the roof keeps water out. Two quotes for "a metal roof" can describe completely different joints at completely different prices, and the seam is where that difference lives.
Two ways to think about water
There are really two philosophies. One keeps water out by the shape of the joint. The other manages water after it reaches a fastener or an overlap. Keeping it out is more reliable than managing it later, and every system below sits somewhere on that line.
Exposed fastener
Panels overlap at a rib and screws go through the face of the metal into the deck, each with a rubber washer sealing its own hole. The joint is a lap held down by screws. This is the least expensive metal roof and the fastest to install, and it has a long history on barns and pole buildings.
Here's the tradeoff. The seal is a few hundred small rubber washers sitting in the sun, and the panels expand and contract under them every day. The washers age faster than the panel does. Somewhere around year ten to twelve they need checking and replacing. I have yet to find an exposed fastener design that is impervious to the elements, whatever the fastener is called. We will not install this system below a 4/12 pitch, because the slower the water moves, the more time it spends on those screws. Details on the exposed fastener page.
Snap-lock standing seam
The panel edges are formed so one side snaps over the other, with the fasteners hidden under the seam on concealed clips. No screws in the weather, and faster to install than a machine-seamed panel. On a steep, well-draining roof it works well.
The limit is that the seam is snapped, not closed. Standing water, packed snow, ice, and wind-driven rain can all find the open side of the joint. It is not a universal standing seam answer. It performs best where water moves off continuously and worst where it sits, which is exactly where a Midwest winter tests a roof.
Mechanically seamed standing seam
The panel legs stand up 1.5 inches and a seaming machine folds them shut over concealed clips, a single fold or a double fold depending on the slope. There is no exposed fastener to fail and no washer doing the sealing. The clips let the panel move with temperature, which every metal roof has to do, without tearing at a fixed point.
This is our 4Ever Armored, and it is my most trusted standing seam system for residential homes. The folded seam is what lets it handle low slopes and complex roofs that give other systems trouble, and for very low slopes we build the same panel with a tighter Vice Lock fold. It costs more than snap-lock and takes a crew that owns and knows the seaming equipment. Not all standing seam performs the same, and the machine is the difference. More on standing seam.
Interlocking metal shingle
A different shape, same idea. Each steel shingle locks to its neighbors on all four sides, so the roof reads as a traditional shingle line from the street while the joints limit where water can enter by design rather than by gasket. Our 4Ever Fortress works this way. It needs a 3/12 pitch or steeper and costs about what mechanically seamed standing seam costs. Details on metal shingles.
Stone-coated steel, by contrast, uses overlapping formed panels with open water channels underneath. It is designed to manage water after it gets in, which is why I am cautious recommending it for long-term residential use.
How to use this when you compare quotes
Ask each contractor four things: is the fastener exposed or concealed, is the seam snapped or machine-folded, what is the minimum pitch the manufacturer allows, and what maintenance does the system call for. The answers sort the quotes faster than the prices do.
Who each one fits
Exposed fastener fits a tight budget on a steep roof, with a homeowner who accepts the maintenance. Snap-lock fits a steep, simple roof where water never sits. Mechanically seamed fits complex roofs, low-slope sections, and anyone whose main goal is to not think about the roof again. The interlocking shingle fits the homeowner who wants that performance with a shingle look and has the pitch for it.
None of them is universally right. But now you know what you are comparing when someone hands you a quote for "a metal roof."
