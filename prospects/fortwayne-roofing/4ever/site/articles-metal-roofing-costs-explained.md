Why Metal Roofing Costs More Up Front, and Less Over Time
What drives a metal roof's price, why there is no honest per-square number, how the 30-year math works, and when asphalt is the right answer anyway.
Let me start with the part nobody likes: a metal roof usually costs more the day it goes on than an asphalt roof does. That is true and I am not going to talk around it. What I can do is explain where the money goes, what moves the number from one house to the next, and how to compare it honestly against the roof you would buy instead.
Why it costs more up front
The material. A steel panel with a factory PVDF finish is a more expensive product than a bundle of shingles that is, by weight, mostly limestone filler. You are buying steel, a coating tested to last decades, and the trim and flashing to match.
The labor. Metal is less forgiving than asphalt. Every valley, sidewall, and chimney gets formed metal flashing instead of a shingle lapped over a strip of step flashing. Panels get roll-formed to length and seamed with a machine. A crew that does this well has been trained and certified to do it, and that crew costs more per day than one that does not.
The time. A metal roof takes longer to put on than shingles on the same house. Flashing is where the hours go.
What moves the number between houses
We do not publish a per-square price and I would be skeptical of anyone who does without seeing the roof. What makes two metal roofs on two similar-sized houses price very differently:
The system. Exposed fastener is the least expensive. Snap-lock standing seam costs more. Mechanically seamed standing seam and interlocking metal shingles cost more again and sit near each other. How the seam is formed explains what you get for each step.
Roof complexity. Valleys, dormers, hips, chimneys, skylights, and pitch changes each add flashing work. A simple gable is the cheapest shape to roof in any material.
What the tear-off finds. Rotten decking at the eaves, delaminated sheathing, an attic that has never been ventilated. These show up as line items, and a quote should price them as unit rates so a surprise has a known cost.
Access and pitch. Steep, tall, or tight sites take longer and need more equipment.
The math over 30 years
Here is the comparison that matters. Asphalt is a cycle, not a solution. A shingle roof lasts 12 to 20 years, so over 30 years most homes buy it two or three times. Roofing costs rise about 3 percent a year, roughly 30 percent a decade, and each asphalt replacement tends to cost 45 to 60 percent more than the one before it.
Run that out and the cheaper roof is not cheap. The illustration in our homeowner presentations puts a standing seam roof at about $46,031 over a 30-year window against about $151,640 for asphalt, once you count the second and third shingle roofs at their inflated prices. Those are illustrative figures for one house, not a quote for yours, and they leave out energy savings and any insurance discount that would tilt it further. The point is the shape, not the exact number: one purchase against three rising ones.
So the comparison is not one metal roof against one asphalt roof. It is one metal roof against two or three asphalt roofs, each pricier than the last. Divide what you pay by the years you get, for both, and the sticker price stops being the interesting number.
When asphalt is the right answer
If you are selling within a few years, do not buy a metal roof. You will not recover the premium in this market, and you will be paying for a decision the next owner benefits from. Good asphalt is the rational choice for a short hold, and I will say so.
If the lowest number this year is the only question, metal is not your roof, and nobody should pressure you into it.
If the budget is a repair budget, a roof with one failed detail and life left in it is a repair contractor's job, not a replacement.
On financing
Financing changes when you pay, not what it costs. It can make sense for a home you plan to stay in. The four questions to ask any lender, fixed versus promotional rate, total cost, prepayment penalty, whether it is tied to the contractor, are on the financing page.
When you have written quotes side by side, how to evaluate a metal roofing quote goes through what a complete one names and what a thin one leaves out.
The question to sit with
What matters more to you here, the lower number this year or the total cost of keeping a roof over your head for the next 30? There is no universally right answer. If this is a short-term home, asphalt may genuinely be the smarter buy. If you plan to stay, the math usually points the other way.
Now you have the information to decide.
