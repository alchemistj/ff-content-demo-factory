A river town with older roofs
Defiance sits where the Auglaize meets the Maumee, and the older parts of town have river-town housing — two-story homes with steep pitches, real valleys, chimneys, and porches added later at a shallower slope. On those roofs the field is rarely the problem. Valleys, sidewalls, and the chimney are. If the last roofer solved a sidewall with caulk instead of a formed counterflashing, that joint had a service life of a few years and you are probably past it.
Those are good candidates for metal, but the labor is real. An interlocking metal shingle keeps the look and removes the nail heads that failed last time. Standing seam handles the low-slope porch roof a shingle cannot. A quote that treats that house like a simple gable is a quote to be suspicious of.
Weather here is repetition, not one storm
Northwest Ohio winters freeze and thaw over and over. Summer heat softens asphalt and cools it again thousands of times until the granules let go. Wind across the open ground outside town lifts tabs on the exposed side. None of that is dramatic. It is the same cycle until a material that was never built for it gives up.
Metal does not soften, has no granules to lose, and has no tab to lift. It still forms ice at a cold eave; the membrane at the eave and the attic ventilation are what prevent the damage.
Barns, outbuildings, and the house
The machine shed usually already has screw-down panel on it, and that is the right roof for a shed. The washers under every screw wear out around year ten to twelve — a maintenance item on an outbuilding, a problem on the house. On the house, a concealed-fastener system, and never exposed fastener below 4/12.
Who this is not for
If you plan to sell in a few years, asphalt is the rational choice. If one detail is leaking on a sound roof, you need a repair contractor — we do not take repair calls, and I would rather say that than quote a roof you do not need. If you want shingles again, you want a different contractor.
