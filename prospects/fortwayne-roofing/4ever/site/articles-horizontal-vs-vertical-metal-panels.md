Horizontal vs. Vertical Metal Panels: Why Direction Matters
Why metal roof panels on a house run up the slope, where horizontal panels show up, and what each orientation does with water and snow.
People ask me this one after shopping for a carport or a metal garage, where the brochure offers a "regular" roof and a "vertical" roof at two prices. On a house, the question almost never comes up, because there is only one right answer. It is still worth understanding why, because it explains a lot about how a metal roof keeps water out.
The two orientations
Vertical panels run from the ridge down to the eave, parallel to the slope. Every rib, every seam, and every lap runs the same direction the water does. This is how every metal roof we install is laid out, and how almost every residential metal roof anywhere is laid out.
Horizontal panels run side to side, parallel to the eave. You see them on the cheapest prefab carports and "regular style" metal buildings, where the panel is simply rolled over the frame. The ribs cross the slope, so water has to climb over each one on its way down.
What vertical does with water
Water on a vertical panel has a clear channel from the top of the roof to the gutter. It never has to cross a seam. A standing seam roof takes that further by raising every panel joint off the surface and folding it shut, so there is no horizontal joint anywhere on the roof. Panels are roll-formed to the full length of the run for exactly that reason.
Snow behaves the same way. On a vertical panel it slides straight down. On horizontal ribs it catches, sits, and melts into the lap.
Where horizontal panels fail
Every place two horizontal panels overlap is a lap running across the flow of water. On a steep roof with good drainage that can hold up for a while. Add a shallow pitch, a Midwest winter, or wind pushing rain uphill, and water finds the lap. Those laps are also where the fasteners are, so the screw hole and the seam are in the same place the water wants to be.
Horizontal panels are also shorter, which means more laps, more fasteners, and more joints to maintain. They are cheaper because they use less material and less labor. That is the whole tradeoff.
Why this is not really a residential choice
On a house with a pitched roof, solid decking, and valleys, there is no horizontal option worth quoting. Panels go up the slope. Where this question genuinely applies:
Prefab carports and metal garages. The "regular" roof is horizontal with rounded corners. The "vertical" or A-frame roof costs more and sheds water and snow better. In Indiana, Ohio, and Michigan I would pay the difference, because the snow load and the melt-and-freeze at the laps are exactly what the cheaper roof handles worst.
Agricultural and pole buildings. Vertical panels over purlins are standard. If someone proposes horizontal panels on a barn, ask them how the laps drain.
Wall panels. Horizontal panels are common and appropriate on walls, where water runs off the face instead of pooling on it. Do not let a wall detail get sold to you as a roof detail.
The broader point
Orientation is one example of a rule that runs through all of metal roofing: water should run with the seams, not across them. Horizontal laps, exposed screws in the flow path, closed valleys that carry water over joints, all of those are places where a roof is managing water after it has found a way in. A vertical panel with a locked seam is keeping it out in the first place. Keeping water out is more reliable than managing it later.
If you are comparing two carport quotes, that is the lens. If you are roofing a house, you do not have to choose, and anyone offering you a horizontal panel roof on a home is offering you a building product, not a roof.
Now you have the context to read the brochure.
