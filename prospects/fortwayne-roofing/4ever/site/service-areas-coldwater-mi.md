Coldwater is a lake town and a farm town at once
Branch County gives me two roofs in one county. The chain of lakes south of town — cottages that became year-round homes, decks facing the water, wind with a long run-up across open water. And the farm ground around it, with older farmhouses, newer ranches, and screw-down panel on the barns.
Winter here is colder and a touch snowier than Fort Wayne, and the part that matters is the freeze-thaw on the edges of the season. Snow melts off the warm part of the roof, refreezes at the cold eave, and backs water up. On asphalt it only needs a nail head. On a mechanically seamed panel there is nothing for it to find.
On the lakes
Open-water wind is where fastener choice shows itself. A concealed-clip standing seam roof holds every panel with no screw head in the weather. That is worth more on a shoreline lot than in town. The other lake question is sliding snow — a roof plane letting go over the deck or the walk down to the water is a real event, and snow retention above anything you walk under belongs in the quote from the start, not discovered after the first thaw.
Lake homes also tend to have low-slope additions. Mechanically seamed standing seam handles genuinely low slope; an interlocking metal shingle wants 3/12 or better; we do not install exposed fastener below 4/12.
In town and on the farm
Older Coldwater homes with steeper pitches and valleys suit an interlocking metal shingle if you want the roof to still look like shingles. The barn keeps its screw-down panel — the washers wear out around year ten to twelve, and on an outbuilding you can keep up with that. On the house, I would not.
Who this is not for
If you are selling within a few years, asphalt is the rational choice. If one flashing or boot is leaking on an otherwise sound roof, that is a repair. We do not take repair calls — a repair contractor is the right answer, and nobody should talk you into a replacement to fix one detail.
