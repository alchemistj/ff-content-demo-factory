Why metal roofs leak, and how to find the source
Metal roofs rarely leak through the panel. Where they do — flashing, fasteners, valleys, boots, ice dams — how to trace it, and what a good fix looks like.
A leaking metal roof is almost never leaking through the metal. In fifteen years I can count on one hand the times the panel itself was the problem. It is nearly always a joint — and which joint tells you whether this is an afternoon's work or a sign the roof is finished.
One thing up front, because it should change how you read the rest of this. I install metal roofs. 4Ever does not take standalone repair calls — that is the shape of this business, not a comment on repair work. So nothing below is written to talk you into anything. If your leak is a fixable detail, the useful thing I can hand you is a clear picture of what a good fix looks like.
The stain is not the leak
Start here, because it saves people a lot of wasted effort. Water enters at one point, runs along the underside of the decking or down a rafter, and drops somewhere else. On a low-slope run it can travel twenty feet before it shows up. The ceiling stain tells you where gravity gave up, not where the roof failed. That is why "I patched right above the stain and it still leaks" is such a common call.
One more thing to rule out first: if the water shows up on cold, clear mornings rather than during rain, you may not have a leak at all. Warm indoor air condensing on the underside of cold metal makes an identical drip, and no patching fixes it — that is a ventilation and insulation problem.
Where metal roofs actually leak, in order
Flashing, especially sidewalls. Anywhere the roof meets a vertical surface — a dormer cheek, a chimney, a wall above a porch roof. This is the single most common source. The detail should be formed metal with a counterflashing let into or lapped behind the wall covering. If someone solved it with a bead of sealant, that joint had a service life of a few years.
Fasteners, on exposed-fastener roofs. Every visible screw has a neoprene washer under it doing the sealing. Those harden and shrink — generally around year ten to twelve, sooner on a south-facing slope. A few weeping screws is a repair. Failure across the whole field is the roof telling you something. This aging-washer problem is the main reason concealed-fastener systems like standing seam exist — the screws never see weather. Overdriven screws are their own version: crushing the washer at installation destroys the seal, and those leak from year one.
Valleys and penetrations. Valleys carry water from two roof planes into one channel, so any debris dam or short flashing shows up fast. Pipe boots crack with UV — a rubber boot is a ten-to-fifteen-year part on a fifty-year roof, and replacing one is routine maintenance.
Ice-dam backup at the eaves. The Midwest one. Snow melts against a warm deck, refreezes at the cold overhang, and water backs up under the roofing. If the self-adhered membrane at the eave was run well past the interior wall line, nothing happens. If it stopped short, you get a stain every February and the roof above it is fine.
The panel itself. Last, and rare. Usually it traces to installation — panels cut with an abrasive saw throw hot filings that embed in the finish and rust in place. The panel was fine until someone put a grinder near it.
What you can check yourself
From the ground with binoculars, and from inside the attic — I would rather you not go up on the roof. Look at the attic underside during or right after rain; wet decking, rusty nail tips, or a stained rafter puts you much closer to the entry than the ceiling stain does. From the ground, look at every place the roof meets a wall, and at the chimney — visible sealant is a finding. On an exposed-fastener roof, look for screws sitting proud or crooked, and streaking below them.
Repair or replace: the honest version
This is the question underneath every leak call, so here is how I weigh it.
A repair makes sense when the failure is local and has a name: one sidewall flashing done in caulk, a cracked pipe boot, a handful of washers on the slope that takes the afternoon sun. If the panels and coating around it are sound and the decking underneath is dry, fixing that one detail solves the problem.
Here's the tradeoff. A repair fixes the detail that failed — it does nothing for the identical details next to it that are the same age. Replace six washers at year twelve and the other six hundred are still twelve years old. A sealant patch buys a few years at best; a formed-metal fix lasts, but it costs more and takes someone who knows how to bend flashing. None of that makes repair wrong. A repair is a decision about one detail, not a reset for the roof.
The signals pointing the other way are pattern and substrate: the same failure repeating on every slope, heavy chalking with rust creeping in at the panel edges, deck rot spreading past the immediate leak. That is not a collection of small problems; it is one big one arriving gradually.
The limitation worth naming: nobody can make this call from the driveway, including me. It takes eyes on the specific details.
If it is a repair, here is what good work looks like
Say your leak is one detail on a roof that is otherwise fine. There are capable repair contractors in every market I work in. What separates the ones worth hiring is whether the fix rebuilds the joint or covers it.
Sidewall and headwall flashing. A new flashing formed out of metal, with the counterflashing let into the wall or lapped behind the siding so water sheds over the top of it. Sealant belongs behind that joint as a second line of defense, not on top of it as the joint.
Pipe boots. A new boot sized to the pipe, bedded in butyl, fastened with gasketed screws. A bead of caulk around a cracked boot is a way to see the same leak next spring.
Weeping fasteners. Failed screws replaced a size up so the new threads bite fresh metal, with a new washer, driven until the washer just spreads to the edge of the head. Re-driving the original screw tighter crushes whatever is left of the washer.
Valleys. Debris cleared, and if the valley metal is short or the panels were cut too tight against it, that section gets rebuilt. Sealing a valley that is dimensionally wrong only moves the problem.
Ice-dam backup. Opening the eave and running self-adhered membrane well past the interior wall line. Anything cheaper is managing the symptom.
Two questions worth asking whoever quotes it: what is the fix made of, and how long do you expect it to hold? Ask for before-and-after photos of anything you cannot see from the ground. And if the roof is under a manufacturer warranty, read the terms first — an uncertified repair can void coverage that would have paid for the work.
When replacement is the better math
The clearest case is an exposed-fastener roof at twenty to twenty-five years. Every screw went in the same week with the same washer, and those washers age on the same clock. Re-screwing the whole field a size up is a real option while the coating is still doing its job; it stops being worth it when the finish has chalked out, rust shows at the cut edges, or the holes have wallowed out. Past that you are paying labor to put good screws into tired steel.
The asphalt version has the same shape — granules in the gutters, curling edges, mat showing through. Past a certain age you are not fixing a roof, you are buying months. The honest comparison is cost per year of protection, not repair price against replacement price.
What I will not do is pretend every leak is a replacement. A sound roof with one bad flashing is not an upgrade case no matter how it gets framed. If that is your roof, the right answer is a repair contractor.
Where that leaves you
If you can trace the water to one of the details above, you already know more than most callers do. Most leaks are repairs. That is the likeliest outcome, and it is a good one.
If what you have looks more like the second case — an aging screw-down roof, or a roof at the end of its run — an assessment is worth having, and it may well come back saying this is a repair. That answer costs you nothing, and it is the one I would rather give. The repair-or-replace guide walks through how I read a roof's condition.
Now you have the context to decide.
