Are Metal Roofs Loud in the Rain?
Where the "loud metal roof" idea comes from, what actually drives roof noise on a house, and the three cases where metal really is more audible.
This is the objection I hear more than any other, and the honest answer has two halves. A metal roof on a house is not loud in the way most people picture. A metal roof on a pole barn absolutely is. The difference is not the metal. It is everything underneath it.
Where the picture in your head comes from
Almost everyone who tells me "metal roofs are loud" is remembering a barn, a shed, a carport, or a cabin at the lake. Those are all the same assembly: panels screwed straight to open purlins with nothing between the metal and the air inside. The panel becomes a drumhead with a big empty cavity behind it. Rain on that is genuinely loud, and I would not argue otherwise.
A house is a different assembly. The panels sit on solid decking, plywood or OSB, over underlayment, over rafters, with an attic and a layer of insulation below that. Every one of those layers soaks up energy before the sound reaches you.
What actually decides the noise
In rough order of how much each one matters:
Solid deck versus open purlins. This is most of it. A continuous deck takes the drum cavity away entirely.
Attic space and insulation. A vented attic with insulation at the ceiling is a large acoustic buffer. A cathedral ceiling with the roof right over your head is not.
Underlayment. A synthetic or peel-and-stick layer adds mass and damping between the panel and the deck. Real, but smaller than the two above.
Panel profile. A panel with striations or a stiffening rib is less prone to oil canning and resonance than a broad flat pan.
Independent testing on this generally lands in the same place the Metal Roofing Alliance does: a metal roof over solid decking measures within a few decibels of asphalt over the same deck. That is not a difference most people can pick out without being told to listen for it.
Where I will not tell you it is quiet
Three cases where metal really is more audible, and you should know them before you buy.
Cathedral and vaulted ceilings. There is no attic buffer. You will hear heavy rain and you will hear hail. Most people do not find it unpleasant, but it is noticeable, and if you are sensitive to sound this is the case to think hard about.
Finished attics and bonus rooms directly under the deck. Same reason. Insulation in the rafter bays helps, and it is worth doing for energy reasons anyway.
Hail, on any assembly. Rain is a soft impact spread across the whole roof. Hail is not. A metal roof in a hailstorm is louder than asphalt, and there is no detail that makes that untrue. It also stays watertight afterward, which is the tradeoff you are buying.
What can be done about it
If you are in one of those cases and want to reduce it:
Insulation in the rafter bays, blown-in or batt, does more than anything else you can add.
A high-temperature peel-and-stick underlayment across the field adds mass and damping. The effect is modest, and it improves the water barrier at the same time, so it is rarely wasted money.
A batten system with an air gap above the sheathing is genuinely effective, acoustically and thermally. It is also genuinely more expensive.
What I would not do is pay for a system to solve a noise problem you have not confirmed you have. If the house has a conventional attic, this is very unlikely to be the thing you notice.
Who should weigh this, and who should not
If your house has a vented attic, which most do, noise should not factor into your decision at all. Stand under a metal roof during a rainstorm before you rule it out. The reality rarely matches the expectation.
If you have a vaulted great room, a finished attic, or you already know you are sensitive to sound, take it seriously. It is a real tradeoff. It is manageable with insulation and assembly choices, and it is worth pricing those in rather than discovering them after.
Now you have the context to decide.
