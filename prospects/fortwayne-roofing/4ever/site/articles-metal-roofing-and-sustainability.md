Metal Roofing and Sustainability: The Honest Version
Recycled content, recyclability, the asphalt landfill problem, and the one tradeoff that decides whether the environmental case applies to your house.
The environmental case for metal roofing is real, and I am going to make it in plain terms. I am also going to tell you where it does not apply, because a roof bought for the wrong reason is still the wrong roof.
What asphalt does at the end
Asphalt shingles are a landfill product by design. Around 12 million tons a year go into the ground in this country, and they take centuries to break down. A shingle roof lasts 12 to 20 years, so a house that stays on asphalt sends two or three roofs to the landfill in the time one metal roof stays on. That cycle is the environmental problem, more than any single roof.
What metal does instead
Steel roofing carries recycled content, roughly a third for steel, and closer to 95 percent for aluminum. At the end of its life, a metal roof is scrap, and scrap steel has value. It gets picked up and melted down, not hauled to a landfill. The number that matters is that it is 100 percent recyclable, and in practice it gets recycled because someone will pay for it.
The other piece is time. A roof that lasts 50 to 70 years is a roof that is not manufactured, shipped, and installed two more times. The lowest-impact roof is the one you do not have to buy again.
The tear-off question
Metal can often go over one layer of existing shingles if the deck is sound, which keeps that layer out of the landfill and saves the labor of removing it. We inspect the roof and attic first. I will be honest that I generally prefer to see the deck, because a tear-off is the only time anyone looks at the wood, and a roof over a rotten deck is a roof over a problem. When the deck is verifiably sound, an overlay is a reasonable choice. When it is not, the landfill saving is not worth what you do not find.
Energy, in this climate
A reflective metal roof lowers attic temperatures and cooling load. The commonly cited range is 15 to 30 percent off cooling costs, and in a hot climate that is a real line on the bill. In Indiana, Ohio, and Michigan we heat more than we cool, so the yearly saving is smaller than the brochure number. It is still a saving, and the energy article goes through what actually drives it.
Where the case breaks down
Here's the tradeoff. A metal roof costs more the day it goes on, and the environmental return, like the financial one, arrives over decades. If you are selling in five years, you pay for the material and the next owner gets both the savings and the lifespan. That is not a reason to buy it. If the home is a short hold, good asphalt is the rational choice, and it is not a moral failing to pick it.
I also do not think the environmental point should decide a roof by itself. It is a real point in metal's favor if you are already leaning toward a long-term home. It is not a reason to spend the money if the rest of the decision points the other way.
The part that is not about the roof
Some of what gets sold as "green" is the roof doing a job the attic should be doing. Ventilation and insulation save more energy than roof color does, and they work under any roof. If efficiency is the goal, fix those first. A metal roof over a badly ventilated attic is still a metal roof over a badly ventilated attic.
Who this applies to
If you plan to stay, the roof outlasts you, and one install replaces two or three, the environmental case is straightforward and it is yours. If you are moving, it is someone else's. Long-term homes deserve long-term thinking.
Now you have the context to weigh it.
