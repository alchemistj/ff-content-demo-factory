What Maintaining a Metal Roof Actually Involves
No roof is zero maintenance. What a metal roof asks of you each year, what not to do to it, and how the answer changes by system.
No roof is maintenance-free, and anyone who tells you metal is one is selling, not explaining. What is true is that a well-built concealed-fastener metal roof asks for less than almost anything else you can put on a house, and it asks for it less often. Here is what it actually wants from you, and what it wants you to leave alone.
What changes by system
An exposed-fastener roof has a real maintenance schedule: a look at the screws and washers every year or two, and a fastener service somewhere around year ten to twelve. That is the tradeoff for the lower price, and it is the whole reason I steer long-term homeowners toward something else.
Standing seam and interlocking metal shingles have no fasteners in the weather. Maintenance on those is mostly about keeping water moving and noticing the rare thing that changes. Stone-coated steel adds one note: the granule surface weathers visually over decades, which is separate from whether it is watertight.
The yearly look
Most of this can be done from the ground with a decent pair of binoculars, or from a ladder at the eave. I would rather you not walk a metal roof. It is slick when wet or frosty, and the hardware you would damage by walking on it is the hardware keeping water out.
Valleys and behind chimneys. Leaves and needles pile up, hold water against the panel, and dam it sideways under the roofing. Clearing these once or twice a year is the single most useful thing a homeowner can do. Most valley leaks I hear about are debris on a roof that was built right.
Gutters. A full gutter backs water up under the eave trim. Same fix.
Sealant at penetrations. Pipe boots, vent flashings, and satellite mounts rely on sealant, and sealant has a service life. Cracked or pulled-away sealant at a boot is a small job now and a ceiling stain later.
Anything touching the roof. A tree branch rubbing on the finish wears through it. Trim it back.
After a storm. Look for lifted trim, a displaced ridge cap, or debris. A cosmetic hail dent is not a leak and does not need action.
What not to do
Do not pressure wash at the seams. You will drive water where it was never meant to go. A garden hose from the top down is plenty if you want it clean, and most roofs never need it.
Do not put dissimilar metals on it. Copper flashing, copper gutters draining onto steel, treated lumber against the panel. Galvanic corrosion is slow and certain.
Do not let someone cut on it with an abrasive saw. The hot filings land on the finish and rust in place. This is where most "my metal roof is rusting" stories come from.
Do not screw things into it. Satellite dishes, holiday light clips, solar mounts. On standing seam, attachments clamp to the seam without a penetration. On any system, a hole in the field is a future leak.
Do not paint a factory finish. A PVDF coating is good for decades. Field paint is a three-to-five-year cycle. If a roof has faded to the point someone suggests painting it, the conversation is about what coating it had and how old it is, not about paint.
Moss, algae, and staining
Metal does not feed organic growth the way asphalt does, and a smooth PVDF surface sheds it. On a shaded north slope under trees you can still get some. A soft wash with a mild detergent handles it. Tannin streaks from overhanging trees wash off. Rust streaks are a different thing and point to a bare cut edge or a foreign piece of steel, a dropped screw or a filing, and they are worth finding the source of.
When to call someone
If the roof is sound and one detail has failed, a boot, a flashing, a few washers, that is a repair contractor's work, and it is a good outcome. If an exposed-fastener roof is losing washers across the whole field, or a roof has reached the end of its run, that is a different conversation, and the repair-or-replace guide walks through how to tell which one you have.
The honest summary
A concealed-fastener metal roof wants a look each spring, clear valleys and gutters, and for you to leave it alone otherwise. That is less than a shingle roof wants and far less than a shingle roof gives you in return. It is not nothing, and I would rather you know that now.
Now you know what the roof is asking of you.
