Metal Roofs and Mold: Where Growth Actually Happens
Why metal does not feed mold, what grows on a shaded roof surface anyway, and the attic condition that causes the mold homeowners actually find.
Mold comes up in two different ways when people ask about metal roofs, and they are not the same question. One is whether stuff will grow on the roof. The other is whether a metal roof causes mold in the attic. The answers are different, so let me take them separately.
On the surface
Asphalt shingles feed organic growth. The limestone filler in modern shingles is food for the algae that makes those black streaks on north-facing slopes, and the rough granular surface holds moisture and gives moss a grip. That is why shingle manufacturers sell algae-resistant lines and why people run zinc strips along their ridges.
Metal gives growth nothing to eat and not much to hold onto. A smooth PVDF-finished panel sheds water and dries fast, and there is no organic material in it. On most roofs, most of the time, a metal roof stays clean.
The honest exception is shade. A north slope under a heavy tree canopy stays damp, collects leaf litter, and the litter holds moisture and decays. Moss and algae can get a start in that, on any roofing material. On metal it is a surface film, not something rooted in the roof, and a soft wash with mild detergent takes it off. Keeping the valleys and the slope clear of debris is the prevention.
Tannin streaks under an oak or a walnut look like growth and are not. They wash off in the rain. Rust streaks are a different thing and mean a bare cut edge or a dropped piece of steel somewhere uphill, which is worth finding.
In the attic
This is the mold homeowners actually find, and it has almost nothing to do with what the roof is made of.
Attic mold grows when warm, moist air from the house reaches a cold roof deck and condenses on the wood. The moisture comes from inside: bathrooms, kitchens, a dryer vented into the attic, a damp crawl space. The cold surface is the underside of the deck in winter, under any roof. If the attic cannot dry, the deck stays wet and mold grows on it.
Metal sometimes gets blamed because a panel gets cold quickly, which is true and beside the point. The deck under asphalt gets cold too. What decides the outcome is the assembly: air sealing at the ceiling so the moist air never gets up there, balanced intake and exhaust so what does get up there leaves, and insulation at the ceiling plane so the attic stays cold and dry. The condensation article walks through each.
Where a metal roof helps, and where it does not
It helps in one specific way: a re-roof is the best moment anyone will have to fix the ventilation, because the deck is exposed and the ridge is open. Every install of ours starts with the attic. If your attic has mold now, it should be addressed before a new roof goes on, not covered by one.
It does not help if the deck is already wet and nobody changes the assembly. A metal roof over an attic that cannot breathe is a long-lasting roof over a deck that is slowly failing underneath it.
The pole barn picture
People who have seen water dripping off the underside of a barn roof on a cold morning sometimes assume a house will do the same. A barn has no ceiling, no insulation, and no deck. Warm air meets cold steel directly. That is a building with no assembly, and it is not what a house is.
Who should think about this
If you have a shaded roof under trees, expect to keep it clear and expect to wash a north slope now and then, with any roofing material. If you have had attic mold, or a damp attic, fix the air sealing and ventilation first and make sure whoever re-roofs the house looks in the attic before they quote. If neither of those is you, mold is not a reason for or against a metal roof.
Now you know where the growth actually comes from.
