Sheridan's houses are older than the rest of Hamilton County
Most of the Hamilton County work we do is on homes built from the 1990s onward. Sheridan is different. FEMA's structure inventory puts the typical home in town in the late 1950s, and the roughly 1,100 single-family homes here include a lot of farmhouses and older ranches.
That changes what I am looking at before anyone talks about panels or colors.
What is under the old roof matters more than the new one
On an older house, the questions are usually about what we will find on tear-off: how many layers of shingles are up there, whether the decking is plank or plywood and what shape it is in, and whether the framing was ever built for the load it is carrying now. None of that can be answered from the driveway, and it is where most budgets on older homes fall apart.
Metal is lighter than asphalt, which helps an older structure. But a metal roof installed over bad decking inherits the problem, and a careful tear-off costs money that a quick re-roof skips. Here's the tradeoff: you pay more up front to find out what is really there, and in exchange the new roof is not sitting on something that will fail first.
Barns, shops and outbuildings
Around Sheridan, the house is often not the only roof. Exposed-fastener panels are common on barns and shops for good reason: they cover a lot of area at a lower cost. The limitation is real, though. The screws use rubber washers that wear over time, so it is a roof you inspect and re-tighten, and we will not install it under a 4/12 pitch. For the house, standing seam or a metal shingle is usually the better long-term fit.
When metal is the wrong call here
If you are planning to sell, if the budget is really a repair budget, or if the leak is one detail on an otherwise sound roof, metal is not the answer, and we do not take repair work. If this is a long-hold property, it is worth the conversation. Our Noblesville office serves Sheridan. This is usually where the decision becomes clear.
