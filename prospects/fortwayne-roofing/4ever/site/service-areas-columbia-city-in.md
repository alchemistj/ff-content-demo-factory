Whitley County is two kinds of roof
Columbia City is close enough to Fort Wayne that a lot of people who live here work there, and the housing shows it: older homes around the courthouse square with steep pitches, dormers and additions, and newer subdivisions on the edges with simple hip and gable roofs. Then the county opens into farm ground and the lakes north of town.
Those are different jobs. The older in-town houses are all about flashing — valleys, sidewalls, chimneys — and the deck underneath, which is often plank that has been wet. The newer roofs are straightforward. The farmsteads usually come with a barn already wearing screw-down panel, and that roof is the first thing people point at when I pull in.
The barn roof and the house roof are not the same question
Exposed fastener is real metal and an honest product. Every screw goes through the panel with a rubber washer under it, and those washers wear out around year ten to twelve. On a shed or a pole barn, that tradeoff is often worth the lower cost. On the house you plan to keep, I usually steer people to a concealed-fastener system — a mechanically seamed standing seam on a long simple run, or an interlocking metal shingle if you want it to read as shingles from the road. We do not install exposed fastener under 4/12.
Winter here is Fort Wayne's freeze and thaw with a little more snow. Ice at the eaves is a normal condition. The membrane at the eave and the ventilation in your attic prevent the damage; the panel just makes the consequences smaller.
When I tell Columbia City homeowners not to do this
If you expect to sell within a few years, good asphalt is the rational choice, and I will tell you that on the phone. If one flashing or one boot is leaking on a sound roof, that is a repair — we do not take repair calls, and a repair contractor is the right call. If the smallest number this year is the question, metal will lose that comparison.
