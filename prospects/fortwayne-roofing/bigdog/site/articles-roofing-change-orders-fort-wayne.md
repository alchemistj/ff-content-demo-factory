Roofing Change Orders: Avoid Surprises in Fort Wayne
By Big Dog Roofing · Published September 20, 2026
A roofing change order records a change to the work you already agreed to: what is different, why it is needed, how the price changes, and whether the schedule changes. For Fort Wayne homeowners, the best time to agree on this process is before tear-off. If extra work is proposed during the project, ask for the finding, supporting photos, and an updated scope you can understand before giving approval through the agreed process.
An unexpected condition is not automatically a sign of a bad estimate. It also does not explain an added charge by itself. A useful change order connects the condition found on your roof to a specific repair and a clear adjustment to the project total.
Know what the original roofing scope includes
Start with the version of the proposal you accepted. Keep it accessible on your phone or in a project folder, along with any product selections and written revisions. When someone calls about additional work, you should be able to find the relevant section without searching through several estimates.
The Federal Trade Commission's home-improvement guidance recommends written estimates that describe the work, materials, completion date, and price. That gives you a useful starting point for reviewing a later change. Our guide to reading a roofing estimate covers the original proposal; a change order should explain the difference from that agreed scope.
Look for allowances, exclusions, and any unit pricing for work that cannot be fully measured beforehand. Ask the contractor to explain those terms in plain language. An allowance included in the original total and an additional charge above that allowance should not be confused.
Separate hidden conditions from optional upgrades
Roof decking sits below the roof covering, so its full condition may not be visible before removal. GAF's roof-decking explanation notes that problems discovered during reroofing can require repairs and an amended quote. It also explains that installing a new roof does not automatically require replacing all the decking.
That distinction matters when reviewing a roof decking change order. Ask which areas are affected, what condition was found, and why the proposed replacement quantity is appropriate. A close-up photograph can show a problem; a wider photograph or marked roof sketch can help locate it.
An optional product change is a different conversation. If you decide to change a color, accessory, or other selection, have that request identified separately from concealed damage. This keeps the record clear about what the roof needed and what you chose to add. Neither category should be buried in an unexplained “extras” line.
Ask for evidence you can review from the ground
You do not need to climb onto the roof to review proposed work. Ask the crew's designated contact for photographs and an explanation. Stay outside the work zone, and let trained personnel inspect exposed roofing and decking.
A useful evidence set connects three things: location, condition, and proposed correction. For example, a photo labeled “rear slope near chimney” is easier to understand than an unlabeled close-up of wood. Ask whether the finding is confined to the photographed area or whether further inspection is still underway.
If the contractor cannot yet determine the final quantity, ask how the uncertainty will be handled. Will you receive a revised count before more work proceeds? What is already known, and what remains to be exposed? Recording that distinction avoids treating a preliminary observation as a final measurement.
Review quantities and the revised total together
A unit price has limited value without a defined unit and quantity. If a proposal refers to sheets, boards, or linear feet, ask what each line measures. Confirm which material is proposed and what the quoted work includes, such as removal, installation, and disposal.
Compare the added work with any amount already included in the original scope. Ask the contractor to show the calculation rather than leaving you to infer whether an allowance has been credited. The change should make the new project total easier to follow.
Use this short review list:
The original proposal or contract reference.
The location and reason for the change.
The added or removed work, including materials and quantities.
The additional charge or credit and revised total.
Any effect on the schedule or payment milestones.
The approval record and any items still awaiting a decision.
This is a practical review checklist, not a claim that every contractor uses the same form. A document labeled “revised estimate” may serve the communication purpose, but you should be able to distinguish it from the superseded version.
Set a contact and protection plan before tear-off
Before a roof replacement starts, decide who can approve changes and how the contractor will reach that person. If you will be at work or away from your Fort Wayne home, provide a workable contact method and discuss what happens if you cannot respond immediately.
Ask how the contractor will keep the home protected while a decision is pending. Also ask which work can continue and which work depends on the new approval. The right sequence depends on the actual roof condition and the work underway; a homeowner should not direct the crew to leave an exposed area unattended.
For a fall roofing project in Northeast Indiana, include the effect on the remaining workday and any revised completion plan in that conversation. You do not need a predicted storm to establish a communication plan. You need a named contact and a clear explanation of what happens next.
Keep insurance paperwork separate from work approval
If an insurance claim is involved, distinguish the contractor's proposed work from the insurer's coverage decision. Ask which photos and scope documents will be provided for review, who is submitting them, and what response is still pending. Added work appearing in a contractor's estimate does not establish that an insurer has approved payment.
Keep a copy of each version rather than replacing the original paperwork. Big Dog Roofing's insurance claim help concerns storm documentation and contractor scope; it does not guarantee coverage or a particular claim outcome. Questions about policy coverage belong with the insurer or the appropriate insurance professional.
Match the final invoice to the approved changes
At closeout, compare the original project total, approved additions, and any credits with the final invoice. Ask about differences while the paperwork is together. Keep the supporting photos and completion notes with that record, especially for work that is now covered by new roofing.
A change-order file is useful later if you sell the home, arrange maintenance, or need another contractor to understand what was replaced. Our roof replacement closeout checklist can help organize the rest of the project documents.
Frequently asked questions
Does a roofing change order mean the original estimate was wrong?
Not necessarily. Concealed conditions can become visible during work. Ask how the new finding differs from the original scope and what evidence supports the proposed correction.
Should extra decking work have a separate price?
Ask for enough detail to understand the quantity, applicable unit price or total, included labor and materials, and how any original allowance is applied. The format should make the revised total clear.
Can I review a change order if I am away from home?
Discuss that before work starts. Agree on a contact method, how photos and revised documents will reach you, and how approval will be recorded. Keep your own copy.
Does my insurer automatically pay for additional roofing work?
No automatic payment should be assumed. Contractor documentation and insurer coverage review are separate steps. Confirm the claim status and your obligations before relying on reimbursement.
