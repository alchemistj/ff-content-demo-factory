How to Document Roof Storm Damage With Photos That Help
Good storm photos show what happened, where the damage sits, and how the condition changed. They do not require you to climb onto a dangerous roof.
By Big Dog Roofing · Published August 9, 2026 · Updated August 26, 2026

The best roof-storm photo set starts wide and moves closer. Record the date and weather. Photograph every side of the home from the ground. Capture visible roof changes, gutters, siding, trees, and debris. Then photograph interior stains or dripping water with enough room in the frame to show where they are. Safety matters more than a close-up.
Photos do not prove insurance coverage by themselves. They preserve conditions so the carrier, roofer, and homeowner can compare what existed before temporary work and what was found during inspection. Pair them with written notes and the steps in our Fort Wayne after-hail guide.
Photo group	What to capture	Why it helps
Storm context	Street conditions, hail size beside a common object, fallen limbs, and the time or weather alert.	Connects the property condition with the reported event.
Whole elevations	Front, rear, and both sides of the home from stable ground.	Shows location and overall condition before close-ups.
Damage details	Missing shingles, bent metal, damaged gutters, torn siding, and debris impact.	Shows the specific condition without losing context.
Interior and attic	Ceiling stain, wall stain, wet insulation, and the roof area above it when safely visible.	Records water entry and how far it traveled.
Repair sequence	Before, during, and after temporary or permanent work.	Preserves hidden conditions and completed scope.
Start With the Event, Not the Shingles
Write down the storm date, approximate time, and what you observed. Save weather alerts and carrier claim information. Photograph hail on the ground before it melts if you can do so safely. Use a coin, ruler, or another familiar object for scale without making claims about what the hail must have done to the roof.
Take a slow video from the yard while narrating the side of the house and visible changes. Video is useful for orientation, but still photos are easier to label and compare. Keep the original files. Messaging apps and social platforms can compress images and remove details, so do not rely on a shared copy as your only record.
Photograph the Exterior From Safe Ground
Stand far enough back to capture each roof plane and elevation. Then move closer for gutters, downspouts, siding, window screens, fence surfaces, roof debris, and visible shingle loss. Use optical zoom or binoculars rather than a ladder. Mark the direction in your notes: north slope, rear elevation, garage eave, or left side when facing the home.
Soft-metal damage on vents, gutters, or downspouts can help an inspector understand the storm path, but it does not prove the shingles are damaged. Photograph it as one part of the property record. Avoid chalking, lifting shingles, or creating test marks yourself. The professional inspection should distinguish storm-created conditions from age, installation issues, and ordinary wear.
Record Interior Water Without Losing Location
Take one wide photo of the entire room, one medium photo showing the stain on the ceiling or wall, and one close photo showing texture or active moisture. Place a container under drips and move valuables. If water is near electrical fixtures, keep your distance and shut off the affected circuit only if you can do so safely.
In the attic, photograph from a stable walkway. Show the rafter or truss bay, stained decking, wet insulation, and any visible daylight. Do not step between framing members. Water can travel along decking and rafters, so the wet ceiling may not sit directly under the roof opening. Our roof leak source guide explains that path.
Document Emergency and Tear-Off Conditions
Before a tarp or emergency repair covers damage, take wide and close photos if the area can be reached safely. Save the emergency invoice and description of work. After temporary protection, photograph the finished tarp, fasteners, and covered area from the ground. The goal is a clear sequence, not a dramatic image.
During tear-off, hidden decking or multiple layers can disappear within minutes as the crew moves. Ask the contractor to photograph the condition before removal, after removal, and after replacement. Each photo should include a reference point or caption. A tight picture of damaged plywood means little if nobody can tell which roof plane it came from.
How to Build a Roof Storm-Damage Photo Record
Record the storm Save the date, time, alerts, ground conditions, and hail or debris from a safe location.
Photograph every elevation Take wide images of the front, rear, and both sides before moving closer.
Capture visible details Use zoom from the ground for missing material, bent metal, gutters, siding, and impact points.
Document interior water Take wide, medium, and close photos of stains, drips, wet insulation, and safe attic views.
Preserve the repair sequence Photograph conditions before temporary work, during exposed tear-off, and after completion.
Label and back up the files Keep originals in dated folders and note the elevation or room for each group.
A Photo Quality Check Before You Submit
Can someone locate it? Include an elevation, room, roof plane, or nearby feature.
Is there context? Pair the close-up with a wider view rather than sending an isolated texture.
Is the file original? Retain the full-resolution photo with its original date information.
Does the caption stay factual? Describe what is visible without guessing the cause or coverage.
Is the sequence complete? Include before, exposed condition, and completed work when repairs hide evidence.
What Changes in Fort Wayne and Northeast Indiana
Fort Wayne storms can bring hail, straight-line wind, heavy rain, falling limbs, and fast temperature changes in the same system. Walk the property only after lightning and high wind have passed. Watch for downed lines, broken glass, loose branches, and siding or metal edges that can cut you.
Winter documentation has another problem: snow and ice can hide the roof while water appears inside. Photograph the roofline, ice buildup, attic moisture, and interior stains without climbing. A later dry-weather inspection can compare those records with flashing, ventilation, and shingle conditions.
What the Inspection Should Show
For storm-damage photos, a useful report should locate and photograph the conditions that affect the decision instead of offering only a repair-or-replace conclusion.

Mistakes That Make the Problem Harder
Do not climb a wet, icy, steep, or visibly damaged roof for a closer picture.
Do not edit, filter, or annotate the only copy of an original image.
Do not label normal wear as hail damage; describe what is visible.
Do not discard damaged material until the carrier says it is no longer needed for review.
Frequently Asked Questions
Do I need roof close-ups before filing a claim?
No. Start with safe ground photos, storm information, and interior damage. A qualified inspector can capture roof-level details later. Do not risk a fall to create a claim file.
Should I photograph hail next to a coin?
A familiar object can provide scale, but keep the image factual. Hail size on the ground does not by itself establish what happened to every roof component.
Can a contractor send photos directly to my insurer?
Yes, with your authorization, but keep your own copy of the complete report and submitted files. You should know what evidence is being used on your claim.
What if the leak appears days after the storm?
Photograph it when discovered, note the date, protect the interior, and notify the appropriate parties promptly. Water can travel or take time to become visible through insulation and finishes.
