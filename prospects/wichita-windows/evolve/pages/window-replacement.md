## Signs Your Windows Are Costing You

Most people don't replace windows on a whim. Something tips them off. These are the problems we hear about most, and what each one usually means:

- **Curtains move when the window is shut.** Air is getting past worn seals or a frame that has shifted. You're paying to heat and cool the outdoors.
- **Fog or haze between the panes.** The seal that holds the insulating gas between the panes has failed. The window has lost most of its insulating value, and it won't clear up on its own.
- **One room is always hotter or colder than the rest.** Old single-pane glass and thin frames let summer sun and winter cold straight through. West-facing rooms feel it first in a Kansas July.
- **You can hear the street, the neighbor's dog, the train.** Sound comes in through the same gaps air does. Sealed, multi-pane glass quiets a house more than most people expect.
- **Sashes that stick, won't lock or won't stay open.** Worn balances and warped frames. In an older house it's rarely just one window.

If it's one or two windows, replace those and stop there. If it's most of the house, we'll show you what doing it all at once costs next to doing it in stages, and you choose.

## Window Styles, and Where Each One Earns Its Place

We install casement, double hung, sliding, awning, hopper, picture, bay and basement windows. The right one depends on the opening and how you use the room.

- **Double hung** is the familiar up-and-down window, and it fits most older Wichita houses without changing how the front looks.
- **Casement** windows crank out on a hinge. They seal tightly when closed and catch a breeze when open.
- **Sliding** windows suit wide, low openings where there's no room to swing a sash.
- **Awning and hopper** windows hinge at the top or bottom, so you can leave them open a crack without letting rain in.
- **Picture** windows are fixed glass: the most light and the fewest parts to wear out.
- **Bay** windows push out from the wall and give a living or dining room its view.

At your consultation you also choose colors, hardware and grids, so the windows look like they belong to your house.

## Fewer, Bigger Windows Where They Make Sense

Older Wichita houses were often built with rows of small windows: three narrow units where one wide one would do.

When we measure, we look at every opening and ask whether it should stay the way it was built. Combining two or three small windows into one larger one gives you more glass and a cleaner look from the street.

It also means fewer windows to buy and install, which can take a real bite out of the price of the job. We show you both versions and you decide.
