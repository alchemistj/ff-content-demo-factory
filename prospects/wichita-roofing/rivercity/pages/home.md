## What Your Roof Is Telling You

Most roof calls start with something you can see. Here's what usually causes it, and what we do about it.

**A stain on the ceiling after a rain** Water runs along the decking and down a rafter before it drips, so the stain is often several feet from the opening. We start in the attic, follow the trail up to the roof, and fix where the water gets in, which isn't always the shingles.

**Shingles in the yard after a wind storm** Wind breaks the seal strip under a shingle, then peels it off the next time it blows, and the felt underneath soaks up every rain after that. We replace what's missing and check the shingles around it, because a broken seal can look fine from the ground.

**A drip near the chimney or a plumbing pipe** Flashing around chimneys pulls loose, and the rubber boots around pipes crack in the sun years before the shingles wear out. Either one is a small repair that costs a fraction of a roof, once someone finds it.

**Curling shingles and grit in the gutters** Sun, wind and hail have worn the granules off, and the shingles underneath are giving out across the slope. Patches only buy a season at that point, so we write a detailed estimate for a new roof in composition shingles, metal or tile.

**Bare spots or dents after hail** Hail bruises shingles and knocks the granules loose, and those spots often start leaking a year later. After a bad storm it's worth having someone on the roof even when nothing looks wrong from the driveway.

**Water spilling over the gutters** Clogged or sagging gutters push water back under the roof edge and into the fascia boards. We repair gutters and install gutter guards, and we'll tell you if the roof edge behind them has started to rot.

**How do I know if I need a repair or a new roof?**

Age, spread and cause decide it. A roof with one failed spot, like a flashing or a vent boot, is a repair. A roof with curling, bare patches and granules in the gutters across the whole slope is at the end of its life. If you've already repaired it more than once, a new roof usually costs less than the third repair and the water damage between them.

**Can I check my roof myself?**

From the ground, yes. Look for missing or lifted shingles, granules collecting at the downspouts, and stains or damp insulation in the attic with a flashlight after a rain. Don't climb a wet or steep roof; call and we'll go up.

**How long does a roof last?**

Asphalt shingles generally give you 20 to 30 years, less on a hot, south-facing slope or after hail. Metal and tile run longer. Good attic ventilation and clean gutters add years to any of them.

**What decides the price of a new roof?**

Size and pitch come first, then the material you pick and how much old decking needs replacing once the old roof is off. Extra layers, chimneys, skylights and valleys add labor. That's why we write a detailed estimate after we've seen the roof, rather than quoting a per-square-foot number over the phone.

**How long does a replacement take?**

A typical house with asphalt shingles is usually a one-to-three-day job for a crew. A big, steep or cut-up roof, or one with rotted decking, takes longer, and rain can push the start date.

**Will my insurance pay for it?**

It depends on your policy, your deductible and what caused the damage. Wind and hail are the usual claims. Check your policy for a separate wind and hail deductible, take dated photos of the damage, and keep receipts for any temporary fixes. We can't promise what your insurer will do.
